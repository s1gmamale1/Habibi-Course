## PART A — Per-letter "listen-for" mistake lists

### A.0 Structural framework: Makharij (articulation points) and Sifat (characteristics)

Classical tajweed organizes correction around two axes, both of which the `/teach` listen-for lists should use as columns:
- **Makhraj** — WHERE the sound is produced (17 points across 5 regions per Ibn al-Jazari's traditional count: chest/jawf, throat/halq, tongue/lisan, lips/shafatan, nasal cavity/khayshum).
- **Sifat** — HOW it's produced (voiced/unvoiced (jahr/hams), strong/weak (shiddah/rakhawah), raised/lowered tongue (isti'la/istifal), heavy/light (tafkheem/tarqeeq), and unique traits like qalqalah, safeer, and daad's istitalah).

A **Lahn Jali** (major/clear error — wrong makhraj or a "lazima" sifah, changes meaning, obvious to any listener) vs **Lahn Khafi** (minor/subtle error — wrong "aaridha" sifah like madd length, ghunna duration, tafkheem degree; doesn't change meaning but degrades quality) distinction is the standard scoring axis used in competitions and should map directly to how checkpoint rubrics weight errors (see Part B).

Sources:
- Lahn classification: [Lahn In Tajweed – Lahn Jali And Lahn Khafi With Examples – Shaykhi Academy](https://shaykhi.com/blog/lahn-in-tajweed/), [Major and Minor Mistakes in Tajweed (Lahn Jali & Khafi) — Mubarak Academy](https://mubarakacademy.online/en/major-and-minor-mistakes-in-tajweed/), [A Teacher's Guide to Tajweed Errors — Hifz Focus](https://hifz.iqs.org.in/blog/tajweed-error-categories)
- Makharij/Sifat overview: [Makharij of Arabic Letters — KALIMAH](https://kalimah-center.com/makharij-of-arabic-letters/)

### A.1 Throat letters (al-huruf al-halqiyyah): ء ه ع ح غ خ

Three sub-zones, deep→upper in the throat: {ء hamza, ه haa} deep; {ع ayn, ح haa} mid; {غ ghayn, خ khaa} upper.

| Letter | Listen-for mistake | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| **ء (Hamza)** | Sound too weak/swallowed to be heard, especially when stopping on a word ending in hamza; incorrectly made "heavy" (tafkheem) when it's adjacent to an emphatic letter | Beginners don't fully close and release the glottis; heaviness bleeds in from neighboring letters |
| **ه (Haa - light)** | Applying unwanted heaviness near heavy letters (e.g. in النهار); vocal cords separated too much → sound too breathy/weak to carry | Confused with English "h", which varies more in strength than Arabic ه |
| **ع (Ayn)** | **Substituting hamza** (ء) — flattening the throat squeeze into a plain glottal stop; producing it from the "heavy alif" position instead of mid-throat; sound cut off early when it carries sukoon | No equivalent phoneme in English or Turkic languages; the muscular throat-squeeze is not habituated |
| **ح (Haa - heavy)** | **The single most-cited non-Arab error**: substituted with خ (khaa) or with the light ه — "non-Arabs frequently substitute Khaa or Haa sounds instead [of proper ح]" | ح's precise mid-throat friction point with hams (whispered/unvoiced airflow) has no counterpart in English, Turkish, Uzbek, Persian, or Urdu |
| **غ (Ghayn)** | Confused with خ (shared upper-throat origin, only voicing differs); blended toward ق (qaaf); described as close to a French uvular "r" but must stay a throat fricative not a stop | Uvular/guttural fricatives are rare cross-linguistically |
| **خ (Khaa)** | Losing its heaviness (tafkheem) — pronounced too lightly, especially before kasrah, where students flatten it toward a plain "h" or "kh" as in Persian/Turkish loanwords | Turkic/Persian "x" sound is close but not identical; students transfer the native sound and under-emphasize |

Sources: [Common Mistakes in Pronouncing Arabic Letters — Bayyinah](https://bayyinah.com/arabic-letters/), [Throat Letters in Arabic — KALIMAH](https://kalimah-center.com/throat-letters-in-arabic/), [Makharij of Arabic Letters — KALIMAH](https://kalimah-center.com/makharij-of-arabic-letters/)

**Uzbek-specific inference (unverified, flagged):** Uzbek/Turkic and Persian/Urdu phonologies lack pharyngeal consonants (ع, ح) and the voiced uvular fricative (غ) as native phonemes; Uzbek does have a velar/uvular fricative close to خ in some registers (borrowed from Persian/Arabic vocabulary, written "x"), so خ may transfer reasonably well while ع, ح, and غ will likely need the most explicit throat-muscle coaching. Verify this against the actual student's L1 accent in lesson 1.

### A.2 Emphatic letters (huruf al-isti'la / mufakhkham): ص ض ط ظ (+ ق خ غ)

The four "big four" emphatics ص ض ط ظ each pair with a light counterpart the student already half-knows (س ت/د ذ), which is the main confusion vector.

| Letter | Light counterpart confused with | Listen-for mistake | Correction cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ص (Saad)** | س (Seen) | Pronounced as plain س — loses the "full-mouth," thick, hissing/whistling quality (safeer + tafkheem together) | Should sound like a "thick hiss," mouth cavity filled, not a thin front-teeth hiss |
| **ض (Daad)** | — (no light counterpart; hardest letter) | Weak/absent Istitalah (the lateral elongation unique to this letter); produced as a plain د or a "z"-like sound instead of from the side of the tongue against the upper molars | This is documented as *the single hardest Arabic letter for all non-native speakers*, not Uzbek-specific: many languages (Turkish, Persian, Urdu) don't have this sound at all and substitute a "z" |
| **ط (Taa - heavy)** | ت (Taa - light) | Produced like plain ت, losing the Jahr+Shiddah+Isti'la+Itbaq combination; described as "the heaviest letter in Arabic" | Should have a held/forceful release with a full-mouth echo, not a crisp dental tap |
| **ظ (Zhaa/heavy Thaa)** | ذ (Dhaal) | Produced as plain ذ, losing heaviness; confusable in some Arabic dialects even for native speakers (Egyptian/Levantine merge ظ↔ض) | Combines the "th" articulation of ذ with the full tafkheem of ط/ض |

**The tafkheem "bleeds into the vowel" mistake (documented, cross-population, not English-specific):** a common beginner error is applying heaviness to the emphatic consonant itself but immediately softening the following vowel — the heaviness must carry through the *entire syllable* including any following alif-madd (e.g. in الضَّالِّينَ the alif between ض and ل is heavy only because it follows a heavy letter, not on its own). Also: Alif al-Madd has *no independent* tafkheem/tarqeeq ruling — it fully inherits from the preceding letter.

Sources: [Bayyinah — Common Mistakes](https://bayyinah.com/arabic-letters/), [Tafkheem and Tarqeeq — KALIMAH](https://kalimah-center.com/tafkheem-and-tarqeeq/), [Heavy and Light Letters — Buruj Academy](https://burujacademy.com/blog/heavy-and-light-letters-in-tajweed/)

### A.3 Thaal / Zay / Zhaa / Daad confusion cluster (ذ ز ظ ض)

Documented specifically as a **non-Arab, non-English-native-speaker cluster** (explicitly named for Persian, Turkish, Urdu speakers — directly relevant to an Uzbek speaker, whose L1 has extensive Persian/Turkic contact):
- The search evidence states plainly: *"Speakers of Persian, Turkish, or Urdu pronounce certain Arabic sounds differently because the emphatic 'D' sound (ض) exists hardly anywhere else and becomes just another way to say 'Z' in these languages."*
- Many learners cannot reliably distinguish ز (Zaay, plain z) from ذ (Dhaal, "th" as in "this") — both may surface as a plain "z" in the learner's mouth.
- Even within Arabic dialects, ظ and ض are merged in some regional speech (Egyptian/Levantine colloquial) — so this is a documented hard cluster even for natives, not a marker of poor learning.

**Practical listen-for check for this course:** test the four sounds ذ / ز / ظ / ض in minimal-contrast drill words early in Phase 1 (Unit 1.1, shape-family ذ/ز/ر grouping already planned in the spec) and flag any collapse toward "z" as the primary Uzbek-relevant error to correct explicitly.

Sources: [KALIMAH — Arabic Alphabet Guide](https://kalimah-center.com/arabic-alphabet/), search synthesis citing Persian/Turkish/Urdu D→Z substitution pattern.

### A.4 Other frequently-confused pairs (general, not Turkic-specific but universal beginner errors — include in listen-for lists regardless)

| Pair | Mistake | Meaning-changing example |
|---|---|---|
| ق (Qaaf) vs ك (Kaaf) | Qaaf flattened to plain "k" — loses its deep uvular/guttural "cough-like" quality from the back of the tongue against the soft palate | قال (he said) → كال (he measured); قلب (heart) → كلب... actually the commonly cited example is Iqlab context: mispronouncing changes "qalb" (heart) meaning if shifted toward ك ("kalb" = dog in some citations) |
| ث (Thaa) vs ذ (Dhaal) | Thaa (unvoiced "th" as in "think") pronounced instead as voiced ذ ("th" as in "this"), or vice versa | Changes root meaning in many word pairs |
| ح vs ه vs خ (three-way) | All three collapsed toward a single generic "h"/"kh" | Core throat-letter confusion, see A.1 |

Sources: [Bayyinah](https://bayyinah.com/arabic-letters/), [KALIMAH Makharij](https://kalimah-center.com/makharij-of-arabic-letters/)

### A.5 Nasal/ghunna and noon-sakinah-tanween rule errors (relevant once Phase 3 rules begin)

- Beginners produce ن and م "from the mouth" with no nasal resonance at all; ghunna must audibly vibrate through the nose for the mandated 2 counts. Teacher check: student pinches their own nose while reciting idgham-with-ghunna or ikhfa words — if the sound doesn't change/buzz, ghunna is missing.
- Applying the wrong noon-sakinah/tanween rule (izhar vs idgham vs iqlab vs ikhfa) is described as the most common Phase-3-level stumbling block — beginners tend to default to izhar (clear pronunciation) everywhere because it's the "easiest" not to alter, or to merge everything into idgham.
- Madd errors ("rushing through Madd, or not elongating at all") are called out as "one of the most common and audible mistakes in beginner recitation" — directly relevant to Phase 3's madd family unit.
- Qalb/Iqlab-specific meaning-changing example cited: mixing up ق with ك changes قلب ("heart") to a nonsense/wrong word — useful as a memorable teaching example for why iqlab precision matters.

Source: [Quranica — Complete Guide to Tajweed Rules](https://quranicma.com/tajweed-rules/) (search synthesis), general ghunna/madd mistake documentation across multiple academy sites (convergent, not independently verified against a primary phonetics source).

## PART B — Assessment rubrics from established qaida/tajweed programs

### B.1 Noorani Qaida structure and completion criteria

- The Noorani Qaida (the explicit Phase 1–2 backbone per the spec) has a canonical **17-lesson** structure: Lesson 1 Alphabets → 2 Alphabets with sounds → 3 Joined letters → 4 Muqatta'at letters → 5 Harakat (movements) → 6 Tanween → 7 Letters+movement → 8 Standing fatha/kasra/damma → 9 Maddo-Leen → 10 Movement exercise → 11 Sukoon/Jazm → 12 Sukoon exercise → 13 Tashdeed → 14 Tashdeed exercise → 15 Tashdeed+Sukoon → 16 Tashdeed+Tashdeed → 17 Tashdeed+Haroof Maddah.
- Mastery method at each step (directly reusable as a checkpoint criterion): *"Students pause at each letter, confirm its Makhraj, check the vowel marker, and produce the sound with the correct Sifat before moving forward."* — i.e., a 3-step per-item check: (1) Makhraj correct? (2) Vowel/marker correctly identified? (3) Sifat (heavy/light, nasal, etc.) correctly applied?
- Typical completion timeframe for an adult non-Arabic-speaking learner with an instructor: **1–3 sessions per lesson**, full Qaida in **3–5 months** — a useful sanity-check baseline against this course's own Phase 1+2 timeline (~9 weeks / 26 lessons), which is faster but the course also runs 3x/week live plus daily homework, unlike typical weekly-lesson qaida classes.
- Standard progress tracking: a **mastery chart per page/lesson**, periodic **recorded-recitation review**, and **milestone/monthly reports** to track growth — directly reusable as the "progress lives in localStorage" per-lesson checkbox model already in the spec.

Sources: [The Noorani Qaida Tajweed Book — Learn Quran Tajweed](https://learnqurantajweed.com/blog/noorani-qaida-tajweed-book/), [Teaching Noorani Qaida to Children — Learning Quran Online](https://learningquranonline.com/teaching-noorani-qaida-to-children-complete-guide/), [Noorani Qaida Table of Contents — Islamic Quran Center](https://islamicqurancenter.com/tutorial/noorani-qaida/noorani-qaida-table-content-index/), [Noorani Qaida Lesson 17: Final Mastery Test — Quran Sheikh](https://www.quransheikh.com/noorani-qaidah-lesson-no-17/)

### B.2 Ijazah / sanad correction protocol (the gold-standard live-correction model)

- Ijazah is earned by reciting the **entire Qur'an to a certified teacher** (Fatiha→Nas), who corrects **every** tajweed error in real time, in sessions over weeks/months, transmitting the recitation exactly as received through their own chain (sanad).
- Concrete assessable competencies cited for the ijazah-level exam: correct pronunciation of letters/vowels; mastery of noon/meem rules; mastery of madd rules; ability to sustain precision across **extended** recitation (endurance is explicitly tested, not just isolated correctness).
- A commonly cited passing threshold across programs: **70% minimum** to progress into/through an ijazah track (only one source gave a number; treat as illustrative, not universal).
- **Diagnostic-first workflow** recommended: start with a diagnostic reading session to catalogue the student's specific pronunciation/articulation/timing errors *before* structuring the correction plan — this maps directly onto how Checkpoint 1 should function (diagnose weak letters, don't just pass/fail).

Sources: [Sanad Ijazah Program](https://sanadquran.com/sanad-ijazah-program/), [How to Get an Ijazah in Tajweed — Quran Grace](https://qurangrace.com/how-to-get-an-ijazah-in-tajweed/), [How to Get an Ijazah in Tajweed — Quran Bliss Academy](https://quranbliss.com/how-to-get-an-ijazah-in-tajweed/), [Complete Guide to Get Ijazah — Quran Sheikh](https://www.quransheikh.com/how-to-get-ijazah-in-tajweed/)

### B.3 Lahn Jali / Lahn Khafi scoring — the concrete point system to adapt

This is the most directly reusable rubric mechanism found, drawn from a live Qiraat competition rulebook plus a teacher-facing "error categories" guide. It gives a numeric, defensible scoring scheme:

| Error type | Definition | Point deduction (competition example) | Marking color (teacher-guide example) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lahn Jali** (major) | Wrong makhraj, or wrong "sifat lazima" (permanent/defining characteristics); changes meaning/grammar; obvious to any listener, scholar or not | **-2** per instance | Red |
| **Lahn Khafi** (minor) | Wrong "sifat aaridha" (situational characteristics) — ghunna duration, madd length, tafkheem/tarqeeq degree; doesn't change meaning but hurts quality | **-1** per instance | Yellow |
| Non-fluency | Recitation not fluent/hesitant | **-0.5** | — |
| Self-correction after prompt (bell) | Student catches own error once alerted | **-1** | — |
| Examiner has to correct | Student cannot self-correct | **-2** | — |
| Failure threshold (memorization/qira'at category) | More than 3 examiner-corrected mistakes in one question/passage | Fail that section | — |
| Failure threshold (fluency/naazira reading category) | More than 4 examiner-corrected mistakes in one passage | Fail that section | — |

Teacher workflow from the same sources: (1) mark all Jali errors first since they affect meaning, (2) then assign targeted Khafi practice per specific rule, (3) keep a running error-pattern list per student to spot recurring weak letters/rules rather than treating each session as independent.

**Direct adaptation for this course's 3 checkpoints:**
- Use the Jali/Khafi split as the pass/revise line: **Checkpoint 1 & 2 should fail on any Jali error** (wrong makhraj = the letter/word is objectively wrong) but **tolerate a capped number of Khafi errors** (e.g., madd slightly short, tafkheem slightly under-heavy) with a note to revisit in homework — this matches the spec's existing "pass / revise-these-units" checkpoint model exactly.
- Track errors **per letter/rule, not just per passage**, so "revise-these-units" can name the specific unit (e.g., "revise Unit 1.1 ح/خ/ه" rather than "retake Checkpoint 1").
- For the Final Checkpoint's 4 tests, weight test (1) unseen mushaf page and test (2) hifz recitation with the Jali/Khafi point system above; tests (3) rule-naming quiz and (4) color-mushaf explanation are knowledge checks, better scored as simple correct/incorrect per rule than with the Jali/Khafi scale.

Sources: [Qiraat Competition 2025 — Marking Criteria](https://www.qiraatcompetition.com/marking-criteria/), [Qiraat Competition 2025 — Rules & Guidelines](https://www.qiraatcompetition.com/rules-guidelines/), [A Teacher's Guide to Tajweed Errors — Hifz Focus](https://hifz.iqs.org.in/blog/tajweed-error-categories), [Lahn in Tajweed — Riwaq Al Quran](https://riwaqalquran.com/blog/lahn/), [Lahn in Tajweed — Quranica](https://quranica.com/articles/lahn-in-tajweed/)

### B.4 Concrete checkpoint rubric template for this course (recommendation, synthesized from B.1–B.3)

**Checkpoint 1 (Letters & Sounds, live):**
- Per letter (all 28, all 3 positions): Makhraj correct? (Jali if wrong) / Sifat correct? (Khafi if off) — binary pass per letter, tracked in a simple grid.
- Any voweled syllable read on sight: pass/fail.
- Pass threshold: zero Jali errors on isolated letters; a small capped number of Khafi (e.g., ≤3) triggers "pass with homework flag" rather than outright fail, consistent with B.3's asymmetric weighting.

**Checkpoint 2 (Reading Mechanics, live — "the biggest gate"):**
- Unseen fully-voweled words, then sentences, read unaided.
- Score using the Jali(-2)/Khafi(-1)/self-correct(-1)/examiner-correct(-2) point table from B.3, with a numeric threshold (e.g., ≥70%, matching the illustrative ijazah-track threshold in B.2) or a hard rule of "no Jali error survives an examiner correction."
- Diagnostic-first: log *which letters/rules* caused each deduction so revision targets specific Phase 1/2 units, not a blanket redo.

**Final Checkpoint (4 tests matching the 4 end goals):**
- Test 1 (unseen mushaf page): Jali/Khafi point scoring per B.3, endurance-tested (a full page, not one line) mirroring the ijazah endurance criterion in B.2.
- Test 2 (memorized surahs): same Jali/Khafi scoring, plus a memorization-fidelity axis (omission/addition of words — itself a Jali-tier error per B.3's Jali definition).
- Test 3 (oral rule quiz): simple correct/incorrect per rule named + explained.
- Test 4 (color mushaf): correct/incorrect per color-to-rule mapping identified.

## PART C — Hifz methodology (Sabaq / Sabqi / Manzil) adapted to Fatiha + last 10 surahs

### C.1 The three-part cycle (universal structure across hifz programs)

| Component | Definition | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Sabaq** ("lesson") | The new portion memorized *today* | Fresh material brought to the teacher for evaluation each session |
| **Sabqi** ("recent revision") | The past several days'/1–2 weeks' worth of newly-memorized material | Bridges short-term to long-term; still fragile, reviewed daily/frequently |
| **Manzil** ("old revision") | Everything memorized beyond the recent window — older surahs | Long-term maintenance against the forgetting curve; reviewed on a rotating cycle |

This structure is described as universal across hifz traditions worldwide (only the vocabulary shifts by region — the underlying new/recent/old three-tier review is constant), which is strong justification for adopting it as-is rather than inventing a new scheme for this course.

Sources: [Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil — The Hifz Revision System Explained — Islamic Tuition](https://islamictuition.us/blog/sabaq-sabqi-manzil-hifz-revision-system/), [Sabaq, Sabqi, and Manzil — Suffah Quran](https://suffahquran.com/sabaq-sabqi-manzil-hifz-review-system/)

### C.2 Concrete numbers extracted (directly usable in homework page design)

- **New memorization (Sabaq) session structure:** read the new portion **3 times from the page** with full attention to correct pronunciation → look away and recite from memory (if unable, identify the exact error and repeat) → repeat until reciting without looking → complete **20–40 total repetitions** within the session, alternating look/no-look, until solid.
- **Alternative concrete number:** the "20×20 method" — read a line/verse 20 times looking at text, then 20 more times reviewing = **40 total repetitions**, cited as "usually sufficient" for retention of a single verse.
- **Error-correction specific:** once a mistake is caught, "recite the corrected portion at least **10 times** before moving on" — a concrete number worth building into the homework checklist as its own step (not just "repeat until right").
- **Session order (30-minute reference session):** Sabqi (recent) first → Manzil (older, listen/recite ~8–10 minutes of a 30-minute session) → new Sabaq last. Rationale given: *"Revision happens before new memorization, not after,"* to keep cognitive strength for retention rather than spending it all on new material.
- **Manzil rotation once volume grows:** once a student has 2–3 juz' (or, by analogy, once several full surahs are held), divide all memorized material into **7 sections** and cycle through **one section per day** for weekly full-coverage review. For this course's much smaller final scope (Fatiha + 10 short surahs = 11 items), a simpler daily rotation (e.g., 2 old surahs/day on a ~5-6 day cycle) is a reasonable scaled-down analogue.
- **Daily minimum:** daily practice is the floor; twice-daily is preferred. 30–45 minute sessions are called "sustainable for adult learners" — this matches the spec's own 15–20 min/day homework budget reasonably well if hifz is one part of that budget alongside reading-mechanics/tajweed drills.
- **Pace baseline (not directly applicable but useful context):** general hifz guidance suggests starting at "half a page (~7–8 lines of a 15-line mushaf) per day" for full-Quran memorization — irrelevant in absolute terms for this course (whole short surahs, not partial pages) but confirms "small daily chunk, not whole-surah-at-once" is standard even for capable adult learners.

Sources: [How to Memorize the Quran — Suffah Quran](https://suffahquran.com/how-to-memorize-quran/), [Sabaq, Sabqi and Manzil — Islamic Tuition](https://islamictuition.us/blog/sabaq-sabqi-manzil-hifz-revision-system/), general repetition-method synthesis (20–40 reps, 20×20 method) from multiple academy blogs — convergent across sources, not independently lab-verified.

### C.3 Recommended memorization *order* for Fatiha + last 10 surahs (matches spec's "an-Nas backward" plan)

Multiple sources converge on: start with **al-Fatiha**, then move through **Juz 30 starting from an-Nas working backward toward longer surahs** (i.e., shortest/simplest first) — this is explicitly the sequence the spec already commits to ("short surahs from an-Nas backward"), and it's validated here as matching standard beginner-hifz practice, not an ad hoc choice. One source frames it slightly differently — Fatiha, then the three-ayah surahs (Ikhlas, Kawthar, Asr) first, then the rest of Juz 30 in increasing length order — which is compatible with (a superset refinement of) "an-Nas backward" since the last 10 surahs by mushaf order are already mostly very short and increase only slightly in length moving backward from an-Nas to al-Fil.

Cited beginner motivation benefit: shorter surahs = "faster wins, stronger motivation, quicker usability in daily prayer" — one source claims first-milestone recall within 2–3 weeks of consistent daily practice starting from Juz 30, which is a plausible ballpark check against this course's ~9-week Phase 3 hifz strand running in parallel with the rules strand.

Sources: search synthesis citing Al-Azhar-trained specialists' recommended sequence (specific attributed source: Buruj Academy content surfaced via search — direct URL not independently confirmed, treat claim as illustrative), [What Are The Shortest, Longest, First, And Last Surahs In The Quran — Riwaq Al Quran](https://riwaqalquran.com/blog/shortest-longest-first-last-surahs-in-quran/)

### C.4 Concrete homework-page routine to implement (recommendation synthesizing C.1–C.3)

Each Phase 3 hifz homework page, per surah being newly memorized, should implement:

1. **Sabaq step (new-portion day):** tap-to-hear the qari audio for the new ayah/surah segment → student reads along 3x from the (harakat-full, color-coded) text → student attempts recall without looking, audio available to re-check → repeat until correct → total **20–40 repetitions** logged via a simple tally/checklist the student taps.
2. **Error-correction step:** if a wrong repetition is caught (by student or teacher), the corrected line gets **10 additional dedicated repetitions** before returning to the full-passage flow — a distinct checklist item, not folded silently into the general repeat count.
3. **Sabqi step (next 3–7 days):** the newly-finished surah/segment is added to a rotating "recent" review list surfaced automatically each day until it's promoted to Manzil (teacher/student marks "solid" after some number of clean recitations — a concrete promotion rule, e.g., 3 consecutive clean days, should be decided and stated rather than left vague).
4. **Manzil step (ongoing):** once "solid," the surah joins a rotating pool; with only ~11 items total (Fatiha + 10 short surahs) a simple **daily 2-surah rotation** covers the whole pool roughly every 5-6 days — homework page surfaces "today's manzil surahs" automatically.
5. **Order:** homework checklist always sequences **Sabqi/Manzil review before new Sabaq work**, per the explicit "revision before new memorization" principle in C.2.

This gives the homework pages a real, sourced method rather than an unstructured "practice this surah" instruction, directly satisfying the spec's Job (c) requirement.
