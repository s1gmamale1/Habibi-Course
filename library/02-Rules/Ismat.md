# Al-Iṣmāt — الإصمات

## Definition

**Linguistically**, *iṣmāt* is preventing, silencing, holding back.

**Technically**, iṣmāt is the **heaviness and slowness of articulation** of letters whose
makharij lie away from the tongue's edge and the lips. The classical name records a
lexical fact rather than an acoustic one: these letters are "prevented" from building a
four- or five-letter Arabic root on their own.

## Its opposite

[[Idhlaq]] — ease and speed of pronunciation, from the tongue's edge and the lips.

## Letters

Twenty-three letters — everything outside فِرَّ مِنْ لُبِّ:

ء · ا · ت · ث · ج · ح · خ · د · ذ · ز · س · ش · ص · ض · ط · ظ · ع · غ · ق · ك · ه · و · ي

There is no positive mnemonic; the set is always reached by subtracting the idhlāq six.

## What it sounds like

Like its opposite, iṣmāt is not a sifah a teacher hears on an isolated letter — it has no
sound of its own. What it predicts is **where a learner will slow down**: long words built
from throat and deep-tongue letters cost more breath and more effort, and fluency
collapses there first. A student reading ٱلصَّآخَّةُ or ٱسْتَغْفِرْ at speed will lose
[[Itbaq]] or [[Hams]] before they lose anything in a word built from ف ر م ن ل ب.

## Why it matters

- **The quadriliteral test.** A native Arabic root of four or five letters always contains
  at least one [[Idhlaq]] letter; a word of that length composed only of iṣmāt letters is
  taken as a loanword. This is the whole practical purpose of the pair, and it is a
  vocabulary tool rather than a pronunciation one.
- It explains the *shape* of the syllabus: the letters that need the most drilling —
  ح, خ, ع, غ, ص, ض, ط, ظ, ق — are all iṣmāt letters.
- Pairing iṣmāt with idhlāq keeps the ten-sifat-with-opposites count honest; without it,
  idhlāq would have no partner and the five-pair scheme would break.
