# Al-Līn — اللين

## Definition

**Linguistically**, *līn* is softness, pliancy, ease — the opposite of stiffness.

**Technically**, līn is the emergence of the letter **with ease and no strain on the
makhraj**: the articulators barely approach each other, so nothing is pressed, blocked or
narrowed. The sound glides.

Līn is a *conditional* sifah. It belongs to و and ي only in one specific state, unlike
sifat such as [[Safir]] or [[Istitalah]] which belong to their letters permanently.

## Its opposite

None. Līn is one of the sifat without an opposite.

## Letters

Two letters, **[[Waw|و]] and [[Ya|ي]], sakinah and preceded by a fathah**:

- **وْ** after fathah — خَوْفٍۭ
- **يْ** after fathah — ٱلْبَيْتِ, قُرَيْشٍ

If the same letters are sakin after their homogeneous vowel (وْ after dammah, يْ after
kasrah) they are **[[Madd-Tabii|madd letters]]**, not līn letters. If they carry a vowel of their own
they are ordinary consonants. The three states must be kept apart from the start, because
the length rules differ for each.

## What it sounds like

The teacher listens for a **soft, single, undivided glide** from the fathah into the
sakin letter — one movement, not two sounds.

Two failure modes:

- **Diphthongising** — the student turns وْ into a full English "ow" (as in *how*) or يْ
  into "ay" (as in *day*), sliding the vowel all the way through the glide. Qur'anic līn
  is shorter and flatter than the English diphthong.
- **Inserting a vowel** — a small "u" or "i" appears between the fathah and the sakin
  letter, breaking the syllable in two. This is the more damaging error, since it changes
  the word's rhythm.

Sūrah Quraysh is the ideal drill surah: قُرَيْشٍ, ٱلْبَيْتِ and خَوْفٍۭ put all three
positions of a līn letter in four ayat.

## Why it matters

- **[[Madd-Leen]]** is built directly on this sifah: when a līn letter is followed by a
  letter made sakin by stopping ([[Waqf-Types|waqf]]), it may be lengthened 2, 4 or 6 counts. Without the
  sifah there is no such madd, which is why līn is taught before the madd chapter rather
  than inside it.
- Līn letters are among the four letters some scholars group under *al-khafa'*
  (faintness) — see [[Hams]] for that disagreement. Both descriptions point at the same
  practical risk: these sounds are easy to lose.
- Getting līn right is what stops ٱلْبَيْتِ from being read as a two-syllable "ba-yit".
