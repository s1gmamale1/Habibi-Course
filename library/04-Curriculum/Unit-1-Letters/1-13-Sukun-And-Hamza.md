## Objectives

- Student can read a letter carrying a sukūn as a closed syllable — no vowel added after it, no lengthening — and can name the five qalqalah letters (ق ط ب ج د) and bounce them.
- Student can recognise the hamza in all five of its seats (أ إ ؤ ئ ء), produce it as a clean glottal stop, and keep it distinct from ع in minimal pairs such as عَمَل / أَمَل.
- Student can read roughly 40 fully-vocalised real words containing ع or ح, in initial, medial and final position, holding the correct makhraj — including the hardest case, a bare ع or ح at the end of a word.
- Student can state the beginner-level difference between hamzat al-qaṭʿ (always pronounced) and hamzat al-waṣl (skipped when joined), using الـ as the worked example.

## Hook

Write **بَ** and then **بْ** and ask her to read both.

She can read the first. The second has a small circle instead of a stroke, and there is no vowel to say — the letter just closes. **That is the whole of sukūn, and it is the first mark that takes a sound away rather than adding one.**

Then the trap, immediately, because it is universal: have her read **أَبْ**. Nine adults in ten will say *abu* or *aba*, adding a vowel that is not there. Let her hear herself do it. The clap test — one clap per syllable — makes it obvious without you having to argue.

## What they already know

All three short vowels from Unit 1.3, and the whole alphabet in joined form. Every mark she has met so far has *added* a sound. Today's does the opposite, and that is why it is harder than it looks.

## Teaching sequence

### 1. What changes today

- Until now every letter you read carried a vowel — fatha, kasra or damma. Today two new marks join them, and then you stop reading single letters and start reading WORDS.
- A sukūn ( ْ ) means the opposite of a vowel: this letter has none. Close it and stop.
- A hamza ( ء ) is a sound you have been making your whole life without knowing its name — the catch in the middle of ‘uh-oh’.
- Everything after that is real words, chosen so that the two letters you find hardest — ع and ح — come back again and again.

### 2. Sukūn — the letter with no vowel

- A fatha, kasra or damma OPENS a letter into a vowel: بَ is ‘ba’, بِ is ‘bi’, بُ is ‘bu’.
- A sukūn CLOSES it: بْ is just ‘b’, with nothing after it.
- Two letters joined this way make one syllable you say in a single beat — بَعْ is ‘baʿ’, not ‘ba-ʿa’.
- The mistake to avoid: adding a little vowel after the closed letter. ‘baʿ-uh’ is wrong. Stop the sound dead.
- The other mistake: stretching it. A sukūn never lengthens a letter — that is a different mark you will meet in Phase 3.

### 3. Five letters that bounce — a first look at qalqalah

- Five letters cannot be stopped silently, because your voice-box is closed while you make them: ق ط ب ج د, remembered as قطب جد.
- When one of them carries a sukūn, releasing it makes a small dry echo. That echo is called qalqalah.
- Say مَدْ. You cannot end it without a faint ‘-eh’ escaping. That is qalqalah, and it is correct.
- For now, one rule only: these five bounce, and nothing else does. ط is the one that matters most to you, because it is also a heavy letter.
- The full rules — how strong the bounce is in each position — are a Phase 3 topic. Do not chase them yet.

### 4. The seats of the hamza

- The hamza sound never changes. Only the chair it sits on changes, and the chair is chosen by the vowels around it.
- أ — on an alif. The commonest seat, used at the start of almost every word: أَحَد, أَمَل.
- إ — under an alif. Always means the hamza carries a kasra: إِيمَان.
- ؤ — on a waaw. The waaw is only furniture; you do not say ‘w’: مُؤْمِن.
- ئ — on a yaa, which loses its dots. You do not say ‘y’: بِئْر.
- ء — no seat at all, sitting on the line, usually at the end of a word: ضَوْء, شَيْء.
- You do not need to predict which seat a word uses. You need to recognise all five and read them as one sound.

### 5. Two kinds of hamza: one you always say, one you often skip

- Hamzat al-qaṭʿ (أ) is the ordinary one. You pronounce it every single time, wherever it falls: أَحَد, أَمَل, سَأَل.
- Hamzat al-waṣl (ٱ) is a starter hamza. Think of it as a door, not a sound: if you START there you walk through it and it sounds. If you are already walking, it is not there.
- You have said this thousands of times already. اِسْم on its own is ‘ism’. But بِسْمِ ٱللَّٰه is ‘bismillāh’ — not ‘bi-ismillāh’. The alif of اِسْم vanished.
- The same happens with الـ ‘the’. Alone you say al-ḥamdu. Joined, in بِٱلصَّبْرِ, you say bi-ṣ-ṣabr.
- The test: put وَ in front and read it joined. If the hamza disappears it is waṣl (وَٱسْم → wasm). If it survives it is qaṭʿ (وَأَخ → wa-ʾakh).
- Beginner rule, and it is enough for now: if the alif has a hamza drawn on or under it (أ إ), say it every time. If it is bare and something comes before it, slide past.

### 6. ع is not ء — the difference that changes meaning

- This is the single most common substitution beginners make, and it is not a small one: عَمَل is ‘a deed’, أَمَل is ‘hope’.
- ء comes from the very bottom of the throat, at the vocal cords. Nothing squeezes; the sound simply stops and starts.
- ع comes from the MIDDLE of the throat, and it is voiced. The throat narrows and you feel a squeeze that you can hold: ʿaaaa.
- Test one — stretch it. You can hold a ع for three seconds. You cannot hold a ء at all; it is a catch, not a sound.
- Test two — put a finger on your throat, on the Adam’s apple. On ع you feel a buzz the whole way through. On ء you feel one click and then nothing. Let your finger tell you, not your ear.

### 7. The letters themselves

| Letter | Name | Makhraj | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ء** | hamza | the vocal cords, at the very bottom of the throat (aqṣā al-ḥalq) | You already make this sound constantly in your own speech — the task is not to learn it, but to notice it and write it. |
| **ع** | ʿayn | the middle of the throat (wasaṭ al-ḥalq) | Revision letter — you met ع in Lesson 1.5. Today it is back inside whole words, which is much harder than saying it alone. |
| **ح** | ḥaa | the middle of the throat (wasaṭ al-ḥalq) | Revision letter — you met ح in Lesson 1.2. Today it is back inside whole words. |

**In plain terms:** A small circle means the letter has no vowel — you close it and stop. The commonest mistake is adding a little *uh* after it that was never written.

## Drills

**Sukūn on the hard letters.** Each unit is two letters: the first carries a fatha, the second carries a sukūn. A sukūn means NO vowel — close the letter and stop. Read across, right to left.

**The five bouncing letters — قطب جد.** These five letters (ق ط ب ج د) bounce when they carry a sukūn. Say each one and listen for a small dry echo — never a real vowel.

**Bounce or hold? — ط vs ظ, د vs ذ.** Alternate down the row. Two of these bounce and two do not. If ظ bounces, you have swapped it for a heavy د.

**Hamza in every seat.** The hamza is always the same sound — a clean catch in the throat. Only its SEAT changes: أ إ ؤ ئ, or no seat at all. Name the seat, then read the word.

**ع or ء? — read down each column.** Each pair differs by one letter only. ع is squeezed in the middle of the throat and voiced; ء is a clean catch at the top. Read the pair back to back until they sound different.

**ع inside real words — start, middle, end.** Read right to left. The ع gets harder as it moves toward the end of the word — the last row has nothing after the ع to lean on.

**ح inside real words.** Read right to left. Keep a hand on your throat: ح should feel tight in the middle, with no voice buzzing.

**ح or خ or ه? — read down each column.** Each pair differs by one letter only. These are real, different words — the sound change changes the meaning.

**Hear a reciter say it — real Qurʾanic words.** These are the exact words as they appear in the Qurʾān, tanwīn and all. Tap each one to hear a qāriʾ say it, then copy him out loud.

## Listen-for

The teacher's checklist as it ships. **These are the shipped plain-string form**,
not the structured `ListenForSchema` the tajweed units use — see the banner above.

- An extra vowel escaping after a sukūn (‘baʿ-uh’ for بَعْ) — the commonest sukūn error, and it turns one syllable into two.
- A sukūn stretched into a long vowel. It is the opposite of a vowel; if it lengthens, the madd rules of Phase 3 will land on top of an error that is already fossilised.
- ظ bounced like ط. ظ is NOT a qalqalah letter — if it bounces, she has substituted a heavy د. This is the single most useful contrast in the lesson.
- ء substituted for ع (عَمَل read as أَمَل), the classic Phase 1 error already flagged in the Lesson 1.5 watchlist. Both words are real, so the mistake is invisible unless you are listening for it.
- ع dropped entirely at the end of a word — سَمْع read as ‘sam’, دَمْع as ‘dam’. Nothing follows the ع to prop it up, so it simply disappears.
- ح collapsing into ه once it is inside a word, even when the letter was solid in isolation. Test with بَحْر and صُبْح, not with حَمْد — حَمْد is often right by imitation alone.
- ح hardening into خ under effort, especially when she is concentrating on something else in the word.
- The seat being pronounced: ‘mu-w-min’ for مُؤْمِن, or a ‘y’ audible in بِئْر. The seat is silent furniture.

## Teacher script

- Open by naming the shift honestly: up to now she has been reading letters, and from today she is reading words. That is the whole point of Unit 1.4 — she recognises about 80% of letters on sight, and what remains is production inside real words.
- Teach sukūn before hamza. Sukūn is purely mechanical and gives a quick win; hamza needs more talking.
- Model the sukūn error before the correct form: say ‘baʿ-uh’ deliberately, ask her whether that sounds like one beat or two, then say ‘baʿ’. Naming the error first makes it audible when she does it.
- Diagnostic for a stray vowel: have her say the unit while you clap on the stop. If her stop cannot land on your clap, there is a vowel hiding in there. A phone recording played back at half speed exposes it instantly and costs nothing at home.
- GATE the qalqalah section. Run it only if her plain بَعْ / بَحْ stops are already clean. ‘Add a little bounce’ is heard by a beginner as permission to add a vowel — which is the exact error sukūn exists to prevent. If she is still saying ‘baʿ-uh’, skip qalqalah entirely today and say you will come back to it. A wrong qalqalah is worse than none.
- Keep qalqalah minimal — قطب جد, a dry echo, that is all. If she asks how strong the bounce is, say it depends on position and that Phase 3 covers it. Do not open that door now.
- Sequencing note for you, not for her: the standard Noorani order puts sukūn six chapters after harakat, behind tanwīn and madd. This course pulls it forward deliberately, because you cannot read a single real word without it — بَعْد, عِلْم, شَمْس are all unreadable with harakat alone. The risk that ordering guards against is teaching sukūn alongside harakat; here the harakat grid is already finished and drilled, so that condition does not apply.
- For hamza, start from her own speech: have her say ‘uh-oh’ and then ‘apple apple apple’ slowly, and point out the catch she is already making. Then show that Arabic writes it down.
- The seats are recognition-only today. She does NOT need to predict which seat a word takes — that is a Phase 2/3 spelling rule. Say so explicitly, or she will try to memorise a rule that has not been taught.
- Pay off Lesson 1.12's promise out loud: قَرَأَ appeared there with a note that the hamza would be explained later. Show her the same word again now.
- For ع vs ء run it in two stages. First RECEPTIVE only: you say one of the pair, she points at which it was — six trials, no speaking. Only then production, with her finger on her own throat. The finger does the diagnosis, which hands the check to her instead of keeping it in your ear.
- رَأْس is the keystone item of the whole lesson — a sukūn sitting on a hamza. Close the throat, hold it, release straight into the seen. If she can do رَأْس, both halves of today have landed.
- Do the ع and ح minimal pairs ACROSS, never down the column. Reading down lets her set the sound once and coast; reading across forces a switch on every item.
- Close with the reciter clips. This is the first lesson in the course with real qāriʾ audio on tap — let her hear a fluent voice say the exact words she has just decoded.

## Homework

In the notebook: the sukūn ladder read aloud once daily, stopping every second letter dead. Bounce-or-hold drill (مَطْ / مَظْ / مَدْ / مَذْ) 3× through. The ten hamza words written out with the seat of each one named beside it. The ع word set daily — all three rows, with the final-ع row (سَمْع, دَمْع) read twice as often. The ح word set daily, then the ح/خ/ه pairs read across rather than down. Tap through the seven reciter words once a day and copy the qāriʾ aloud. Watch the 2-minute sukūn video once this week.

- Sukūn ladder read aloud once, every second letter stopped dead
- Bounce-or-hold drill (مَطْ / مَظْ / مَدْ / مَذْ), 3 times through

- ع word set — all three rows, with the final-ع row doubled
- ح word set, then the ح/خ/ه pairs read across
- Seven reciter words tapped and copied aloud
