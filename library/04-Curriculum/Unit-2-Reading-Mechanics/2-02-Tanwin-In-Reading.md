# Lesson 2-02 — Tanwīn in real reading

## Objectives

- Student can read a phrase in which a tanwīn runs straight into the next word, keeping the *n* fully audible and adding no pause.
- Student can stop on a word carrying ـٌ or ـٍ and drop the ending to a bare sukūn — *kitābun* becomes *kitāb*.
- Student can stop on a word carrying ـًا and produce a steady two-count *ā* instead of an *n*.
- Student can read the five verified āyah-endings in this lesson correctly both ways: continuing and stopping.

## Hook

Lead with the contradiction, because the student is about to meet it on her own and it will shake her confidence if you do not name it first:

> "Yesterday I told you the *n* is always there. Today I am going to tell you when it is not."

Play or say the end of Sūrah al-ʿAṣr: **لَفِى خُسْرٍ**. The mark on the page is a kasratān. The reciter says *khusr*. No *n*.

Now say the wrong thing deliberately — *khusrin*, with a hard stop after the *n* — and then the right thing. Then name the rule that explains both: **the ending you see is what you say when you keep going. It is not what you say when you stop.**

This is the first time in the course that the same written word has two correct readings. Say that out loud. It is not an exception or a complication; it is how Arabic has always worked, and it is why reciters can pause anywhere.

## What they already know

- **Lesson 2-01** — the three marks and their sounds, and the silent carrying alif.
- **Lesson 1-13** — sukūn as a closed syllable with nothing after it. The stop-form of ـٌ and ـٍ is *literally* a sukūn, so this is not a new sound, only a new place to use one she already owns.
- **Unit 1.4 generally** — she has been reading real multi-letter words since 1-13, so a two- and three-word phrase is a step up in length, not in kind.

Say the sukūn connection explicitly. "When you stop on *kitābun*, the ending becomes a sukūn — the same mark you learned in 1-13. You are not learning anything new; you are putting it somewhere new."

## Teaching sequence

1. **Continuing first, because it is the default.** Ninety percent of tanwīns in a page of recitation are read straight through. Read a two-word phrase where the tanwīn is in the middle: كِتَابًا جَدِيدًا. The *n* of the first word runs into the *j* of the second with no gap. No breath, no bump. Drill this before you introduce stopping at all — otherwise the student learns stopping as the normal case and starts chopping the text into single words.

2. **Now stopping, in the two easy cases.** ـٌ and ـٍ both become **plain sukūn** at a stop:
   - كِتَابٌ → *kitāb*
   - كِتَابٍ → *kitāb*
   Both stop-forms are identical. Show that on the board — two different marks, one stopped pronunciation. The student will ask how you tell them apart when stopping; the honest answer is that you cannot, and you do not need to.

3. **Then the case that surprises everyone.** ـًا at a stop does **not** become a sukūn. It becomes a **two-count long *ā***:
   - كِتَابًا → *kitābā*
   Here — and only here — the carrying alif you were told to ignore in lesson 2-01 finally does something. It is the one place the alif is pronounced. Name the reason in one sentence: the *n* drops, and the alif underneath it is what is left. Say the name **madd ʿiwaḍ** — "the lengthening of compensation", see [[Madd-Iwad]] — once, so the student recognises it later, and then move on. Its formal treatment is Unit 3.
   - **Length matters here.** Two counts, the same length as any ordinary long vowel. Not three, not one.

4. **The one exception in the stopping rule.** A word ending in tāʾ marbūṭah with fatḥatān — سَاعَةً — does *not* become *sāʿatā*. At a stop the ة turns into a silent *h*: *sāʿah*. Teach this as a spelling fact tied to one letter shape, not as a rule with branches. If she can see a ة, she stops with *h*.

5. **Where you are allowed to stop.** Today: **only at the end of an āyah.** That is a safe, visible, unambiguous boundary marked by a numbered circle. Stopping mid-āyah changes meaning and has its own signs and its own rules, and those come later in this unit. Give her the boundary and the reason, and refuse to be drawn further.

6. **Both ways, on the same word, back to back.** This is the drill that makes the lesson stick and it is worth the last third of the session. Take يُسْرًا: read 94:5 continuing into the next āyah (*yusran*), then read it stopping (*yusrā*). Same word, same page, two correct readings, ten seconds apart.

## Examples

All five are **āyah-final**, which is exactly why they were chosen — each one can be read both ways in a single sitting, and the stop form is the one she will hear from every reciter.

| Word in context | Ref | Continuing | Stopping |
|---|---|---|---|
| هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ | 112:1 | *aḥadun* | *aḥad* |
| لَفِى خُسْرٍ | 103:2 | *khusrin* | *khusr* |
| فَإِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا | 94:5 | *yusran* | *yusrā* |
| قَلِيلًا | 73:2 | *qalīlan* | *qalīlā* |
| أَفْوَاجًا | 110:2 | *afwājan* | *afwājā* |

Two notes for the teacher, not for the student unless she asks:
- **فَإِنَّ** in 94:5 has a shadda. She has seen one before, in ٱلضَّآلِّينَ in lesson 1-14. Tell her to hold the ن and that the mark gets its own lesson in three sessions. Do not teach it here.
- **أَحَدٌ** at 112:4 is written أَحَدٌۢ with a small extra mark. Different rule, Unit 3. If she notices, say so plainly and use 112:1 only.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — run it through, no gap.** Read each phrase as one unbroken piece. The *n* belongs to the first word and touches the second:

كِتَابًا جَدِيدًا · قَلَمًا صَغِيرًا · بَابٌ كَبِيرٌ · دَرْسٌ سَهْلٌ · وَلَدٍ صَالِحٍ · طَرِيقٌ طَوِيلٌ

If you hear yourself take a breath between the two words, start the phrase again.

**Drill 2 — the same word, stopped and continued.** Say the left form, pause, then say the right form. Six pairs:

| Continuing | Stopping |
|---|---|
| كِتَابٌ جَدِيدٌ | كِتَابْ |
| قَلَمٍ صَغِيرٍ | قَلَمْ |
| دَرْسًا سَهْلًا | دَرْسَا |
| بَابًا كَبِيرًا | بَابَا |
| وَلَدٌ صَغِيرٌ | وَلَدْ |
| جَبَلًا عَالِيًا | جَبَلَا |

**Drill 3 — sort them by ear.** Read down the column. For each word, stop on it, and say aloud which ending you produced — "sukūn" or "long *ā*" — before you move to the next:

سَلَامٌ · بَيْتًا · قَمَرٍ · بَحْرًا · صَدِيقٌ · عَمَلًا · مَطَرٍ · نَهْرًا · دَرْسٌ · جَبَلًا

The answer is mechanical: two fatḥas means long *ā*, anything else means stop dead.

**Drill 4 — tāʾ marbūṭah.** Stop on each. All four end in a soft *h*, none in *ā*:

سَاعَةً · مَدِينَةً · وَرَقَةً · شَجَرَةً

**Drill 5 — the real thing.** Read each āyah twice: once running into the next āyah, once stopping at the circle.

1. 112:1 — هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ
2. 103:2 — لَفِى خُسْرٍ
3. 94:5 — فَإِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا
4. 73:2 — قَلِيلًا
5. 110:2 — أَفْوَاجًا

Then listen to a reciter read all five and check your stop form against theirs. Listen for the length of the *ā* in numbers 3, 4 and 5 — count it on your fingers while they recite.

## Listen-for

**1. Stopping on every single word.**
- *Listen for:* a phrase read as a list — *kitāb … jadīd* — instead of as a phrase.
- *Why it happens:* fifteen lessons of single-word drilling. Every word so far has been an island, and nothing has yet required her to join two.
- *Correction cue:* "One breath for the whole phrase." Draw an arc with your finger over both words as she reads, and do not let her start until your finger is moving.

**2. Saying the *n* at a stop — *khusrin*, full stop.**
- *Listen for:* an audible *n* immediately followed by silence.
- *Why it happens:* she is doing exactly what lesson 2-01 taught, correctly. This is a good error and should be praised as one before it is corrected.
- *Correction cue:* "Right mark, wrong situation. When you stop, the ending stops with you."

**3. Stopping on ـًا with a sukūn — *kitāban* clipped to *kitāb*.**
- *Listen for:* a short, closed ending where a long open one belongs.
- *Why it happens:* over-generalising the rule she has just learned for the other two marks — which is what a student who is actually learning does.
- *Correction cue:* "Two fatḥas are the odd one out. That one opens up, it does not close." Point at the alif: "This is the only time that alif gets a voice."

**4. The *ā* at a stop coming out too short — one count, not two.**
- *Listen for:* *yusra* rather than *yusrā*; a stop-vowel no longer than an ordinary fatḥa.
- *Why it happens:* this is the priority error for this student, and it is not about tanwīn at all. Uzbek reached its Arabic vocabulary through Persian, which had already flattened the long/short vowel distinction — so long vowels get shortened across the board. See [[Common-Mistakes]]. It will come back in lesson 2-07 and every lesson after it.
- *Correction cue:* Count it out loud with her — "one, two" — with a finger tap on each. Do not describe the length in words; give her the beat.

**5. The *ā* at a stop coming out too long — three or four counts.**
- *Listen for:* a swelling, sung ending, usually copied from a recording of a slow reciter.
- *Why it happens:* melodic imitation. Reciters in the slow *tartīl* style stretch pauses, and she is reproducing the whole gesture rather than the count.
- *Correction cue:* "Two counts, the same as any long *ā*. The reciter's is longer because he is singing it, not because the rule changed."

**6. Adding an extra pause before the *ā* — *yusr … ā*.**
- *Listen for:* a break between the last consonant and the lengthened vowel.
- *Why it happens:* she is thinking about which rule applies mid-word.
- *Correction cue:* "Decide before you start the word, not in the middle of it." This is the same advice as the self-tests in lesson 1-15 — run the check before the word.

## Homework

**15–20 min/day, five days.**

1. **Four minutes — Drill 1.** All six phrases, one breath each. Record one and check for a gap.
2. **Five minutes — Drill 2.** Both columns, alternating. This is the core of the homework; do it every day even if you skip something else.
3. **Four minutes — Drill 5, the five āyah-endings.** Read each one both ways.
4. **Three minutes — listen.** Play the five āyāt from any reciter you like and count the stop-vowel on your fingers. You are checking one thing only: is your *ā* the same length as theirs?
5. **Once this week — mark up a page.** On a printed page of the last juzʾ, circle every ـًا you can find. Do not read it; just find them. You are training your eye to see the mark before you reach it.

Bring to the next session: whether the *ā* at a stop is coming out too short or too long. Those are two different fixes and you will save the session time by knowing which one is yours.
