# Lesson 2-03 — Sukūn I: harder clusters

## Objectives

- Student can read a word containing two separate sukūns — ٱلْحَمْدُ, وَٱلْعَصْرِ, ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ — without inserting a helper vowel at either one.
- Student can carry a sukūn across a word boundary, so that مِنْ بَعْدِ and أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ are read as one continuous piece.
- Student can hold a sukūn on a letter that leaks — ر ل م ن — for its full length instead of swallowing it.
- Student can read a mixed column of 20 two-sukūn words cold, at a steady pace, and self-check with the clap test.

## Hook

Do not define anything. Say two words:

> "*Al-ḥamdu.* *Al-ha-mu-du.*"

Ask which one she has been saying. Then write **ٱلْحَمْدُ** and count the letters together: alif-lām-ḥāʾ-mīm-dāl. Five letters, and only **two** of them have a vowel.

Here is the thing that has to land today, and it is a habit of the eye, not of the mouth: **a beginner reads the vowels and treats the sukūns as gaps to hurry past. Arabic is the other way round.** The closed letters carry as much time and as much weight as the open ones. مْ in ٱلْحَمْدُ is a whole letter. It is just a letter with nothing after it.

Then the corrective sentence, which you will repeat all lesson: **a sukūn is not a pause and it is not a gap. It is a consonant with the door shut behind it.**

## What they already know

**Lesson 1-13** taught sukūn properly and the student has been reading it for three lessons:

- A fatḥa, kasra or ḍamma *opens* a letter; a sukūn *closes* it. بْ is just *b*.
- Two letters joined that way are one syllable in one beat — بَعْ is *baʿ*, not *ba-ʿa*.
- Adding a little vowel after the closed letter — *baʿ-uh* — is the error, and she already has the clap diagnostic for it.
- A sukūn never lengthens a letter.
- The five bouncing letters ق ط ب ج د were named there. **They come back in the next lesson, not this one.** Keep them out of today's session even if she raises them.

She has also already read words with a sukūn in them since 1-13 — بَعْد, عِلْم, شَمْس, صُبْح, عَظْم — so this lesson is not introducing the mark. **It is increasing the load: more sukūns per word, sukūns on leakier letters, and sukūns that cross from one word into the next.** Say that framing out loud; it tells her why a familiar mark is getting a whole lesson.

## Teaching sequence

1. **Re-run the 1-13 diagnostic first, in sixty seconds.** بَعْ, بَحْ, عَبْ, حَمْ — with the clap. If a helper vowel is still there on a *single* sukūn, stop the lesson plan and drill 1-13's material instead. Everything below multiplies whatever error is already present; there is no point running it over a broken foundation.

2. **Two sukūns in one word.** Build it in stages on the board so the student sees the word assembled rather than presented:
   - حَمْ → *ḥam*
   - حَمْدُ → *ḥamdu*
   - ٱلْحَمْدُ → *al-ḥamdu*
   Each stage read aloud before the next letter is added. The sukūn on the لْ and the sukūn on the مْ are both real; neither one is a gap.

3. **The leaky letters, and this is the genuinely new content.** ر ل م ن و ي are voiced continuants — you can hum them for as long as your breath lasts. That makes them the easiest letters in the alphabet to over-run into a vowel:
   - قَلْب heard as *qal-ub*
   - صَبْر heard as *ṣab-ur*
   - عَصْر heard as *ʿaṣ-ur*
   Contrast with a letter that cannot leak: تْ, كْ, سْ. A سْ ends when the air stops. A رْ ends only when *she* decides to end it. **The rule to give her: a closed ر or ل or م or ن ends the moment your tongue leaves the position — do not let your voice trail after it.**

   **In plain terms:** ر ل م ن و ي are the ones you can hum, which is exactly why they leak — your voice runs on past the letter and grows a vowel you never meant. Cut them dead.

4. **Sukūn across a word boundary.** أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ is *alam-nashraḥ*, not *alam · nashraḥ*. The مْ finishes the first word and the ن starts the second with nothing in between. Teach this as the same thing as Drill 1 of lesson 2-02 — the phrase runs, only now the joining sound is a closed consonant instead of an *n*.
   - The hardest ordinary example in the short surahs is **وَٱنْحَرْ**: a نْ running straight into a ح, two throat-adjacent sounds with no vowel to separate them. Save it for last.

   **In plain terms:** the two words run straight together. One ends on a closed letter, the next starts on one, and nothing goes in between — no pause, no little *uh*.

5. **Three-and-four-sukūn words.** ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ has two; يَسْتَغْفِرْ has three. Read them slowly, syllable by syllable, then at speed. The pace matters: a student who can read a two-sukūn word slowly but not at speed has not finished learning it, she has only decoded it.

6. **Name the boundary you are not crossing.** What happens when a sukūn on the *last* letter of one word meets a sukūn on the *first* letter of the next — that is the next lesson. Say the sentence and stop. Today every cluster is resolvable by reading exactly what is on the page.

**Teacher note on 1:2 and 108:2.** Both contain a shadda (لِلَّهِ, and the doubled letter in ٱللَّهِ). She has met one in 1-14 and will get the full treatment in 2-05. Read it for her rather than explaining it, and keep the attention on the sukūns.

**Teacher note on 94:1.** The final حْ of نَشْرَحْ when stopping, and the دْ of صَدْرَكَ, are both worth pointing at — but دال is a bouncing letter, and that is tomorrow. If she bounces it spontaneously, tell her she is right and that you will name it next session.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — one sukūn, warm-up.** Read across, clap on each stop:

بَعْ · حَمْ · قَلْ · صَبْ · نَشْ · عَصْ · مُسْ · وَقْ

**Drill 2 — the leaky four.** These all close on ر ل م ن. Cut the sound the instant your tongue leaves the position:

قَلْب · صَبْر · عَصْر · نَهْر · بَحْر · شَمْس · عِلْم · فَجْر · حَرْب · سَهْل · جِسْر · أَرْض

Say each word twice: once deliberately wrong, with a helper vowel — *qal-ub* — and once right. Hearing the wrong one on purpose is what makes the right one audible.

**Drill 3 — two sukūns in one word.** Build each one up before you read it whole:

مَكْتَب · مَدْرَسَة · مُسْتَشْفَى · يَسْتَغْفِر · مُسْتَقِيم · تَسْتَعْمِل · مُسْتَعْمَل · يَنْصَرِف

**Drill 4 — carrying the sukūn into the next word.** One breath per phrase, no gap at the join:

مِنْ بَعْدِ · مِنْ قَبْلِ · عَنْ عِلْمٍ · فِى بَيْتٍ · لَمْ نَقُلْ · قُلْ لَهُ · هَلْ مِنْ أَحَدٍ

**Drill 5 — the two throat clusters.** These are the hardest in the lesson because both sides of the cluster come from the throat. Slow, then fast:

وَٱنْحَرْ · مِنْ حَيْثُ · عَنْ عَمَلٍ · لَمْ يَحْمِلْ

**Drill 6 — cold column.** Twenty words, no warning:

صُبْح · وَقْت · بِنْت · عِنْد · قَلْب · كَلْب · شَمْس · نَهْر · حَرْب · مَكْتَب · فَجْر · عَصْر · جِسْر · أَرْض · صَدْر · عِلْم · بَحْر · سَهْل · مَدْرَسَة · مُسْتَقِيم

Time the second pass only. Accuracy first — a slow clean read beats a fast one with helper vowels in it, and always will.

## Listen-for

**1. A helper vowel inside the cluster — *al-ha-mu-du*.**
- *Listen for:* an extra beat. Count the beats on your fingers as she reads; ٱلْحَمْدُ is three, and if you count four there is a vowel in there.
- *Why it happens:* Uzbek, like Persian and Turkish before it, breaks up consonant clusters with a helper vowel — this is one of the most reliably transferred L1 habits and it is documented for the analogue languages. See [[Common-Mistakes]]. It is not carelessness and it will not go away by being pointed out once.
- *Correction cue:* "Two beats, not three." Clap the target rhythm first, then have her read onto your claps.

**2. Voice trailing off a closed ر, ل, م or ن.**
- *Listen for:* a faint *uh* or a hum after the consonant. It is quieter than a full helper vowel and easy to miss — record and play back at half speed if you are unsure.
- *Why it happens:* these letters are voiced and can be sustained, so nothing physically stops them. Every other closed letter stops itself.
- *Correction cue:* "Cut it." Make a scissor gesture with your fingers at the moment of the stop. A physical cue works better here than a verbal one because the fault is a duration, not a sound.

**3. Swallowing the closed letter entirely — *ḥamdu* becoming *ḥadu*.**
- *Listen for:* a missing consonant. This is the opposite failure to number 1 and it usually appears *after* you have corrected number 1 — she over-applies "cut it".
- *Why it happens:* over-correction. The instruction "no vowel after it" gets heard as "make it small".
- *Correction cue:* "The letter is full size. Only the vowel is missing." Have her say the letter alone, held, then in the word.

**4. Pausing at the word boundary — *alam · nashraḥ*.**
- *Listen for:* a gap where the مْ meets the نـ.
- *Why it happens:* a sukūn at the end of a word looks like the end of something, and fifteen lessons of single-word reading taught her to stop at the end of a word.
- *Correction cue:* "The word ends, the sound does not." Trace an unbroken line under both words with your finger while she reads.

**5. Reading the sukūn as a lengthening.**
- *Listen for:* *ṣaaabr*, a stretched vowel before the closed letter.
- *Why it happens:* she is trying to give the closed letter its full weight — the thing you asked for — and the only tool she has for "more time" so far is length.
- *Correction cue:* "Weight, not length. Press it, do not stretch it." This error is worth catching now, because lesson 2-07 gives her a mark that *does* mean lengthen, and the two must not blur.

**6. Losing the sukūns as the column speeds up.**
- *Listen for:* clean reading in the first five words of Drill 6 and mush by word fifteen.
- *Why it happens:* the correct form is still conscious, not automatic. This is a normal stage and it is the reason for the daily homework rather than a longer session.
- *Correction cue:* Do not correct mid-column. Let her finish, then say which word number it started at. Naming the point of collapse is more useful than fixing each word.

## Homework

**15–20 min/day, five days.**

1. **Two minutes — warm-up.** Drill 1 with claps. Every day, without exception; it resets the beat.
2. **Five minutes — Drill 2, the leaky four.** Wrong version then right version for every word. This is the drill that fixes the trailing voice.
3. **Four minutes — Drill 3.** Build each word up letter by letter, then read it whole.
4. **Four minutes — Drill 4.** One breath per phrase. Record two of them and listen for a gap at the join.
5. **Three minutes — Drill 6 cold column.** Once slow, once timed. Write down the time; you are looking for it to come down across the week without the errors going up.

Bring to the next session: the word from Drill 6 that gives you the most trouble, and whether the problem is a helper vowel or a swallowed letter. Those are opposite fixes.
