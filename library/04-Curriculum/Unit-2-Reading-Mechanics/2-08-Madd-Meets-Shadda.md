# Lesson 2.8 — Madd Meets Shadda & Sukūn; Leen Letters

## Objectives

- Student can recognise the maddah sign **ٓ** on sight and knows it means *hold this longer than two counts* before knowing any rule name.
- Student can hold a **six-count** madd, flat and steady, whenever a madd letter is followed by a shadda or a sukūn inside the same word.
- Student can tell a **madd wāw/yāʾ** from a **līn wāw/yāʾ** from an ordinary consonantal wāw/yāʾ, by looking at the vowel that precedes it.
- Student can read a līn letter with **no length at all** when joining onward.

## Hook

Ask her to read **ٱلضَّآلِّينَ** and then **ٱلْحَآقَّةُ**, cold, one after the other.

She will almost certainly give the first one six counts — she learned it in 1-14 — and
give the second one two, or three, or whatever the mouth happens to do. That is the whole
lesson in ten seconds: **she learned a word, not a pattern.**

Say it to her that plainly. "You know ٱلضَّآلِّينَ. You do not yet know *why* you know it.
Today you find out, and then you never have to be taught another one of these."

## What they already know

From Phase 1 and the first half of this unit:

- **Sukūn closes a syllable** (1-13). The mouth stops on the letter.
- **Shadda doubles a letter and is held** (2-05, 2-06). One letter written, two heard: a
  sākin one leaning into a voweled one.
- **ٱلضَّآلِّينَ takes six counts** (1-14) — taught there as a fact about that word, with
  the reason deliberately withheld.
- **The three madd letters** (2-07): ا after a fatḥah, و after a ḍammah, ي after a
  kasrah. Two counts, plain, no decoration. See [[Madd-Tabii]].

Everything below is built out of those four things. Nothing here is a new sound.

> **The 2-07 handover.** 2-07 **identifies** the līn letters and stops there — its student
> instruction is one sentence: *"a و or ي with a sukūn after a fatḥah is read soft and
> short, and what it does when you stop on it is the next lesson."* **2-08 owns treating
> them.** So §4 below is not a repeat: she can already spot them, and today she learns the
> three-way contrast that tells her *why*, and gets the boundary that 2-07 deliberately
> left open.

## Teaching sequence

### 1. The sign comes first, the rule comes later

Open any muṣḥaf and point at the little wave — **ٓ** — sitting over an alif or a wāw or a
yāʾ. Tell her exactly one thing about it:

> **This mark means: longer than two. The printer put it there so you would not have to
> work it out.**

That is the entire first step. She is not learning a rule; she is learning to obey a road
sign. The reason this is worth a step of its own is that the muṣḥaf is doing the analysis
for her, and a reader who trusts the sign reads fluently a year before a reader who
computes.

### 2. Madd + shadda, or madd + sukūn, inside one word → six

Now the one case with a fixed answer. Write these three on the board, unpointed by
explanation:

| Word | What follows the madd |
|---|---|
| ٱلضَّآلِّينَ | آ then **لّ** — a shadda |
| ٱلْحَآقَّةُ | آ then **قّ** — a shadda |
| دَآبَّةٍ | آ then **بّ** — a shadda |

Ask her what the three have in common *before* telling her. She will find it: **a long
alif, then a doubled letter.**

Then give the instruction:

> **Madd letter, then a shadda or a sukūn, in the same word → hold six counts. Always six.
> There is no choice here.**

Flat and steady, not a swell — count it on the fingers, one hand and one finger, and keep
the pitch level. The commonest failure is a madd that starts strong and fades; six counts
of decaying sound is not six counts.

Do not name it. It has a name — [[Madd-Lazim]], "the obligatory one" — and Unit 3 gives it
the name and the sub-types. Here it is a hand-shape and a count.

### 3. Madd + hamzah — also long, count four for now

The other thing the **ٓ** sign sits over is a madd letter followed by a **hamzah**:

- **جَآءَ** — "when the help of Allah *came*"
- **ٱلشِّتَآءِ** — "of winter"
- **يَدَآ**, **إِنَّآ**, **مَآ**, **لَآ** — these are everywhere in the short surahs

Tell her: **the sign means long here too, but the count is four, and Unit 3 will explain
why this one is different.** Say the quiet part out loud — "I am giving you a number
today and the reason later" — because an adult learner who notices an unexplained gap and
is not told it is a gap starts distrusting the whole system.

The two rules behind it are [[Madd-Muttasil]] and [[Madd-Munfasil]]. Do not say those
words in the lesson.

### 4. The three states of wāw and yāʾ

This is the heart of the lesson and the part that pays off for the next six years of
reading. **The same two letters do three different jobs, and the vowel *before* them tells
you which.**

| Preceding vowel | و | ي | What it is | Length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ḍammah** before sākin و | يَقُولُ | — | **madd wāw** | 2 |
| **kasrah** before sākin ي | — | قِيلَ | **madd yāʾ** | 2 |
| **fatḥah** before sākin و / ي | خَوْف | بَيْت | **līn** letter | **none** |
| any vowel *on* the letter itself | وَرَبِّكَ | يَدُعُّ | ordinary consonant | none |

Teach it as a two-question routine she can run out loud:

1. *Does the wāw or yāʾ have a sukūn?* No → it is a plain consonant, done.
2. *What is the vowel before it?* Matching (ḍammah–wāw, kasrah–yāʾ) → **madd, two counts**.
   Fatḥah → **līn, no counts**.

### 5. Līn has *no* length — not "a short one"

2-07 told her not to stretch a līn letter. Today make it exact, because "don't stretch it"
and "it has no length" are not the same instruction and learners settle on the first.

Joining onward, **قُرَيْشٍ** and **خَوْف** and **ٱلْبَيْتِ** carry **no prolongation
whatsoever** — not one count, not half a count. The letter is a glide, and a glide takes
the time the mouth needs to travel and no more. See [[Leen]].

Read Sūrat Quraysh straight through, joined, with no stops except at the end. She should
hear four līn letters go past at ordinary speed.

Then plant the seed for 2-12: *when you stop on one of these, something happens to it.*
Do not demonstrate it yet. Tell her it is coming in four lessons and move on — that is
[[Madd-Leen]], and it needs stopping taught first.

## Examples

Sūrat Quraysh is the drill surah for this lesson, because four āyāt hold every case:

| Āyah | Word | Case |
|---|---|---|
| 106:1 | قُرَيْشٍ | līn yāʾ |
| 106:2 | ٱلشِّتَآءِ | madd + hamzah — four |
| 106:2 | وَٱلصَّيْفِ | līn yāʾ |
| 106:3 | ٱلْبَيْتِ | līn yāʾ |
| 106:4 | خَوْفٍۭ | līn wāw |
| 106:4 | جُوعٍ | **madd** wāw — ḍammah before it, two counts |

That last row is the one to linger on. **جُوعٍ and خَوْفٍ sit eight words apart in the same
āyah**, one a madd and one a līn, and they look almost identical on the page. If she can
read that āyah and give جُوعٍ two counts and خَوْفٍ none, she owns §4.

Six-count set, for the shadda case: ٱلضَّآلِّينَ (1:7) · ٱلْحَآقَّةُ (69:1) ·
دَآبَّةٍ (2:164).

## Drills

**Drill 1 — sort, do not read.** I will show you twelve wāws and yāʾs. Do not read the
word. Just say "madd", "līn", or "consonant". Speed matters more than the word.
Non-Qurʾānic set: بَيْت · نُور · شَيْء · يَوْم · كَبِير · وَلَد · صَوْت · مَدِينَة ·
خَيْر · يَقُول · جَيِّد · سُوق.

**Drill 2 — the six-count hand.** Hold your hand up. Every time you hit a maddah sign over
a letter followed by a shadda, count six on your fingers while you hold the sound. If the
sound gets quieter before your sixth finger, you started too loud. Try again at half the
volume: نَاقَّة · شَآبَّة · مَآدَّة · ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · ٱلضَّآلِّينَ.

**Drill 3 — Quraysh, joined.** Read all four āyāt without stopping until the very end. I
am listening for one thing only: that خَوْف and قُرَيْش and ٱلْبَيْت go past at normal
speed, with no stretch on them at all.

**Drill 4 — the minimal pair.** Say these in pairs, back to back, ten times:
جُوع / خَوْف · نُور / نَوْم · كَبِير / بَيْت. The first of each pair is two counts. The
second is zero. If they sound the same length, you are lengthening the līn.

**Drill 5 — cold sign-reading.** Open the muṣḥaf anywhere in the last juzʾ. Find five
maddah signs. For each one, tell me before you read it: six, or four? (Shadda or sukūn
after it → six. Hamzah after it → four.)

## Listen-for

**Listen for:** a six-count madd that stays at one pitch and one volume from the first
count to the sixth, then releases cleanly into the doubled letter.

- **The mistake:** the madd swells and fades — loud at count one, nearly gone by count
  four, with the last two counts silent breath.
  **Why it happens:** she is measuring the madd by effort rather than by time, and effort
  naturally decays. Untrained singers do the same thing on a held note.
  **Say:** *"Six counts at one volume. Quieter at the start, so you have somewhere to go.
  Don't push — just don't stop."*

- **The mistake:** līn letters get a small stretch — "khaw-wf", "qurayy-sh".
  **Why it happens:** the sākin wāw/yāʾ looks exactly like a madd letter on the page, and
  she has just spent 2-07 learning to lengthen sākin wāws and yāʾs. The rule she built is
  "sākin wāw = long"; the correct rule keys on the *preceding vowel*, which she is not yet
  looking at.
  **Say:** *"Look at the vowel before it, not at the letter. Fatḥah before it? Then it's a
  slide, not a stretch. One movement."*

- **The mistake:** the līn becomes an English diphthong — خَوْف as "how", بَيْت as
  "bait", with the vowel travelling all the way through.
  **Why it happens:** direct transfer from a language whose /aw/ and /ay/ glide fully.
  Qurʾānic līn is shorter and flatter.
  **Say:** *"Start the fatḥah and land on the wāw. Don't travel between them."*

- **The mistake:** a vowel appears inside the līn — "ba-yit", "kha-wuf".
  **Why it happens:** the sukūn is being released instead of held, so the mouth inserts a
  helping vowel to get out of the cluster. This is the more damaging of the two līn errors
  because it changes the word's syllable count.
  **Say:** *"One syllable, not two. Bayt. Say it as fast as you can say it — speed will fix
  it before analysis will."*

## Homework

1. Sūrat Quraysh, read joined, five times a day. Mark the six wāw/yāʾ letters in pencil
   before you start: **M** for madd, **L** for līn.
2. Find three more words in the last juzʾ with a maddah sign followed by a shadda. Write
   them out and count six on each.
3. The minimal pairs from Drill 4, ten times each, aloud. Record yourself once and listen
   back for whether the second of each pair is shorter than the first.
4. **Do not** try to work out what happens when you stop on خَوْفٍ. You will be told in
   2-12. If you have already noticed that it sounds different, say so next lesson — that
   is a good sign, not a mistake.
