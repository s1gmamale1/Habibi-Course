# Lesson 2.12 — Fully-Voweled Sentences I

> **+ Practical stopping is introduced here.** This is the most important teaching
> decision in Unit 2; §1 explains why it sits at this point in the course and not later.

## Objectives

- Student can read a complete fully-voweled sentence in planned breath groups, deciding **before she begins** where she will stop.
- Student can stop correctly: **final ḥarakah dropped**, **ة read as a silent hāʾ**, and a **long vowel before the stop allowed to stretch**.
- Student can restart cleanly from the word after her stop, using the waṣl-hamzah starting vowels from 2-09.
- Student can say what changed about a word when she stopped on it, in her own words.

## Hook

Give her 1:5 and ask her to read it and then stop:

> **إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ**

She will end it *nastaʿīnu* — with the ḍammah still sounding, hanging in the air with
nothing after it.

Say: *"Say that last word again, and then be silent for three seconds."* She will hear it
herself. The word ends on a vowel that is going nowhere. It sounds unfinished, because it
**is** unfinished: no Arabic word ends in a vowel at a stop.

> **"Everything you have read so far, you have read as if something came next. Today you
> learn what a word does when nothing comes next."**

## Why stopping is taught here — read this before teaching the lesson

This course teaches practical stopping in Unit 2, *before* the madd rules that depend on
it. That is a deliberate correction of the classical ordering.

**Madd ʿāriḍ lis-sukūn is defined by what happens when you stop.** It does not exist in
joined reading at all: in waṣl the same word carries a plain two-count [[Madd-Tabii]].
Al-Jazariyyah puts madd in chapter 11 and waqf in chapter 12, and an earlier draft of this
course's own syllabus inherited that order — madd at 3.18, waqf at 3.25. Both put the
consequence before its cause.

So the course fixes it here. **2-12 introduces stopping, 2-13 drills it, 2-14 applies it on
a real page**, and Unit 3.7 then formalises the rule on a foundation the student already
has in her mouth. See [[Madd-Arid-Lissukun]] for the rule as it will eventually be stated,
and [[Waqf-Word-Changes]] for the full nine-item table this lesson takes three items from.

**At this stage stopping is a habit, not a taxonomy.** Teach three things. Do not teach
the counts, do not name the rules, and do not show her the nine-item table — she will meet
all of it in Unit 3, and meeting it now costs her the fluency this lesson exists to build.

## What they already know

- Words (2-10) and phrases (2-11), read joined.
- Breath taken **before** a phrase, and the fact that some phrases do not fit in one
  (2-11 §5). Her list of unfinishable phrases from that homework is the raw material for
  today.
- Sukūn closes a syllable (1-13). Everything in §3 below is that one fact applied at the
  end of a word.
- Madd letters and their two counts (2-07, 2-08).
- How to start on a waṣl hamzah (2-09) — which is what she needs the moment she resumes.

## Teaching sequence

### 1. A sentence is longer than a breath

Put 105:1 in front of her:

> **أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْفِيلِ**

Ask: where will you breathe? Make her answer **before** she reads a word. Then mark the
place with a pencil arc.

This is the single habit the whole lesson is for. The error it prevents — running out of
air mid-āyah and stopping wherever the lungs give out — is the most common and the most
damaging fault in early recitation, because the place the lungs choose is never a place
that means anything.

**The rule for now: stop at the end of an āyah, or at the end of a complete idea.** Where
exactly the permitted stops are, and what the little letters printed above the line mean,
is [[Waqf-Signs]] and [[Waqf-Types]] in Unit 3. Today: āyah ends are always safe. Use them.

### 2. Never stop in the middle of a word

State it flatly, and give the reason, because the reason is what makes it stick:

> **A word broken in half is not a word. Stopping mid-word can change the meaning, and it
> always changes whether what you said is Arabic.**

If she runs out of breath mid-word, the instruction is: **go back to the beginning of that
word and read it again.** Not push through. Not resume from the middle. This is worth
drilling as its own reflex, because the panic response is to continue.

### 3. The three things that happen when you stop

Now the content. Three items. No more.

#### One — the final ḥarakah drops

You stop on a **sukūn**. Whatever vowel was on the last letter, it is gone.

| Joined | Stopped |
|---|---|
| نَسْتَعِينُ | نَسْتَعِينْ |
| ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ | ٱلْعَٰلَمِينْ |
| ٱلرَّحِيمِ | ٱلرَّحِيمْ |
| ٱلْفَلَقِ | ٱلْفَلَقْ |

*Footnote to the same rule, not a fourth rule:* **tanwīn is a ḥarakah too, so it drops the
same way.** أَحَدٌ → *aḥad*. مَسَدٍ → *masad*. The one that looks different is tanwīn
fatḥ — أَفْوَاجًا → *afwājā* — but look at the page: **the alif is already printed there.**
She is not adding anything; she is reading what is written, now that the tanwīn above it
has gone quiet. That framing costs ten seconds and saves a rule.

**In plain terms:** stop on a word and its last vowel just falls off. The printed mark is
for carrying on — when you stop, you end on the bare letter.

#### Two — ة becomes a silent hāʾ

The tāʾ marbūṭah **ة** is read as a **تـ** when you join, and as a **هْ** when you stop.

| Joined | Stopped |
|---|---|
| رَحْمَةً | رَحْمَهْ |
| ٱلْمُوقَدَةُ (104:6) | ٱلْمُوقَدَهْ |
| مَدْرَسَةٌ | مَدْرَسَهْ |

Point at the two dots on the ة and tell her what they are for: **the dots are there for
when you join. Stop, and you ignore them.**

The contrast case matters. **حَمَّالَةَ ٱلْحَطَبِ** (111:4) — she joins straight through,
so the ة is a *t*: *ḥammālata-l-ḥaṭab*. And the exception, once, in passing: **ذَاتَ** in
نَارًا ذَاتَ لَهَبٍ (111:3) is written with an **open** تـ, so it would stop as *dhāt*.
Read what is written; if the tāʾ is open, it stays a *t*.

#### Three — a long vowel before the stop stretches

Read **ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ** joined: the yāʾ of ٱلرَّحِيمِ gets its ordinary two counts.
Now stop on ٱلرَّحِيمْ. **It gets longer.** Let it. Don't measure it, don't count it — just
do not cut it short.

Demonstrate the same word both ways, back to back, twice, and let her hear that the
difference is real and is caused by the stop and nothing else.

Then name the phenomenon and refuse to name the rule:

> **"That stretch happens because you stopped. It is not part of the word. Join onward and
> it disappears again. It has a name and a number of counts, and you will get both in Unit
> 3 — today you only have to let it happen."**

This is the seed of [[Madd-Arid-Lissukun]]. Planting it as an observation now is exactly
why the rule will be easy later.

### 4. Restarting

Where does she resume from? **The word she stopped on, or the word before it** — never
mid-phrase, and never from the middle of a word.

And the moment she restarts, 2-09 is live: if the word begins with **ٱ**, she needs the
starting vowel. ٱلْفَلَقِ resumes as *al-falaqi*. ٱهْدِنَا resumes as *ihdinā*. Do a few
resumes explicitly so she feels that stopping and starting are one skill with two halves.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — plan first, read second.** I give you a sentence. Before you make any sound,
put a pencil arc over each group you will say in one breath. Then read it. If you have to
breathe somewhere you did not mark, we stop and re-mark it — we do not carry on.

**Drill 2 — join it, stop it.** I say a word twice and you give me both readings:
ٱلرَّحِيمِ / ٱلرَّحِيمْ · ٱلْمُوقَدَةُ / ٱلْمُوقَدَهْ · أَحَدٌ / أَحَدْ ·
نَسْتَعِينُ / نَسْتَعِينْ. Say them back to back so you can hear the difference yourself.

**Drill 3 — the ة hunt.** Find eight words ending in ة on this page. Read each one twice:
once joined to the next word, once stopping on it. The dots come and go.

**Drill 4 — stop on my signal.** Read on. When I raise my hand, stop at the **end of the
word you are on** — not immediately, not mid-word. Finish the word, apply the changes,
stop. Then resume when I lower my hand.

**Drill 5 — the mid-word rescue.** I will deliberately make you run short of breath. When
it happens, do not push through and do not resume from the middle: go back to the start of
that word. Practise the going-back until it is automatic, because in a real reading you
will need it.

**Drill 6 — non-Qurʾānic sentences**, so the stopping is read and not remembered:
> ذَهَبَ ٱلْوَلَدُ إِلَى ٱلْمَدْرَسَةِ · فِي ٱلْحَدِيقَةِ شَجَرَةٌ كَبِيرَةٌ ·
> ٱلْمُدَرِّسَةُ تَكْتُبُ عَلَى ٱللَّوْحَةِ · هَذَا ٱلْكِتَابُ لِصَدِيقِي

Every one of them ends in a ة or a tanwīn. Read each joined into the next, then stopping.

## Listen-for

**Listen for:** a clean, silent landing. The last letter closes, the sound stops, and there
is no vowel and no breathy tail after it.

- **The mistake:** the final ḥarakah is still pronounced at the stop — *nastaʿīnu*,
  *al-ʿālamīna*, *aḥadun*.
  **Why it happens:** she is reading exactly what is printed, and the ḍammah *is* printed.
  Nothing on the page tells her to drop it. This is obedience to the text, not carelessness
  — say so, because it is a mistake that deserves credit.
  **Say:** *"The page is written for joining. When you stop, the last mark is not yours.
  Cut it: nastaʿīn."*

- **The mistake:** ة read as a **t** at the stop — *al-mūqadat*, *raḥmat*.
  **Why it happens:** the two dots are right there, and 2-11 spent a whole lesson teaching
  her to read the dots. She is also likely to have heard the *-at* form in ordinary spoken
  Arabic borrowings in her own language.
  **Say:** *"Cover the two dots with your fingernail. Now what letter is it? A hāʾ. Say
  that — al-mūqadah — and let it be soft."*

- **The mistake:** the long vowel before a stop gets cut short — *nastaʿīn* with a clipped
  yāʾ.
  **Why it happens:** stopping feels like *ending*, and ending feels like *hurrying*. She
  is closing the word rather than releasing it.
  **Say:** *"Hold the ee, then close. Let the last sound be the long one — don't run away
  from the end of the word."*

- **The mistake:** stopping mid-word when the breath runs out.
  **Why it happens:** no plan — she started reading before deciding where she would stop.
  It is a planning failure, not a lung-capacity failure, and telling her to breathe more
  deeply will not fix it.
  **Say:** *"Back to the start of that word. Now look ahead and show me where you're going
  to stop, before you take the breath."*

- **The mistake:** resuming from the middle of a phrase, or restarting a waṣl hamzah with
  the wrong vowel — *il-falaq*.
  **Why it happens:** the resume is not being treated as a *start*, so the 2-09 rules never
  get run.
  **Say:** *"You're starting. Which vowel? Is it ٱل? Then it's an a."*

## Homework

1. Sūrat al-Fīl (105), five āyāt. Pencil your breath arcs **first**, then read it once a
   day. Bring the marked copy; I check your plan, not just your reading.
2. Drill 2's join/stop pairs, ten times each, aloud.
3. Find five words ending in ة anywhere in the last juzʾ. Write each one twice, once as it
   is joined and once as it is stopped.
4. Read Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (112) stopping at the end of **every** āyah, then again joining
   every āyah to the next. Four āyāt, two readings. Notice that أَحَدٌ is a different sound
   in each. **You do not have to know why yet.** Just notice it.
