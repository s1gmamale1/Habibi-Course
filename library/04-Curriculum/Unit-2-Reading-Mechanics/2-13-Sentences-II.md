# Lesson 2.13 — Fully-Voweled Sentences II: Fluency

> **+ Practical stopping is drilled here.** 2-12 introduced the three changes; this lesson
> makes them automatic, at speed, across whole surahs.

## Objectives

- Student can read a complete short surah aloud at an even pace, with a breath plan she made herself, and no stop that lands mid-word.
- Student can apply the three stop-changes **without thinking about them** — the test is that she can do them while her attention is on something else.
- Student can recover from a stumble by returning to the start of the current word and continuing, without restarting the surah.
- Student can hear and correct her own stopping errors from a recording.

## Hook

Last lesson she stopped when you raised your hand. Today, hand her the surah and say
nothing.

Give her **Sūrat al-Kāfirūn (109)** — six āyāt, cold, no preparation, no arcs drawn — and
ask her to read it straight through as if she were reading it to someone.

Then, without commenting on the reading, ask her one question:

> **"How many times did you stop, and did you choose any of them?"**

Almost always the answer is: several, and no. The stops happened *to* her. That is the gap
this lesson closes — **2-12 taught her what a stop does; 2-13 makes the stop hers.**

## What they already know

- The three stop-changes (2-12): final ḥarakah drops · **ة** → silent **هْ** · a long
  vowel before the stop stretches.
- Stop only at an āyah end or a complete idea; never mid-word (2-12 §1–2).
- Starting vowels for a waṣl hamzah on resuming (2-09).
- Phrase-level joining and the finger-slide (2-11).
- Six-count madd on madd + shadda (2-08) — which today lands, for the first time, right
  before a stop, in **وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ**.

Nothing new is taught in this lesson. Its content is **repetition under increasing load**,
which is a different teaching job from explanation and should not be padded out with
explanation to feel more substantial.

## Teaching sequence

### 1. She draws the plan, not you

Hand her the surah and a pencil. She marks the arcs. You say nothing until she is done.

Then review the plan **before** she reads a word:

- Does every arc end at an āyah end or a complete idea?
- Is any arc longer than she can hold? (She has last week's list of phrases she could not
  finish.)
- Is any arc so short that it chops the sense — stopping after وَمِن شَرِّ, for instance,
  which is grammatically incomplete?

Correct the plan on paper. Then let her read it. **Planning errors are cheaper to fix
before the reading than after it**, and separating the two also separates the two
skills in her own mind.

### 2. Even pace beats fast pace

Fluency is not speed. It is **the absence of variation in speed**.

The diagnostic is easy: tap a slow, steady beat on the table while she reads. A fluent
reader stays with the beat. A decoding reader speeds up on familiar words and slows on
unfamiliar ones — and the places where she leaves the beat are precisely her weak spots,
visible in real time.

Take her to a speed at which she can hold the beat, even if that is very slow. Then leave
her there for the whole session. Do not increase it. Speed arrives on its own once the
variation is gone; chasing it directly rebuilds the variation.

### 3. Drill the three changes to automaticity

This is the bulk of the lesson and it should feel repetitive. It is supposed to.

The test of automaticity is **whether she can do it while her attention is elsewhere.**
Three loads, in order:

1. **Alone.** Read the surah, stopping at every āyah end.
2. **While counting.** She reads; you hold up fingers between āyāt and she reports the
   number at the end. Her attention is split, and the stop-changes have to survive it.
3. **After a delay.** Read the surah, wait ten seconds in silence, read it again. Skills
   that are only fresh do not survive the silence.

If the ة turns back into a *t* at load 2, it is not automatic yet — it is being
consciously applied, and it will collapse in the checkpoint. Go back to load 1 and do more
repetitions, not more explanation.

### 4. Where the two skills collide — وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ

Al-Fātiḥa's last two words are the hardest thing she has read so far, and they are worth
five minutes on their own:

> **وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ** — a joined waṣl hamzah, then a **six-count** madd, then a doubled
> lām held, then a madd yāʾ, then a **stop** on which that yāʾ stretches.

Four separate requirements landing in one breath. Take it apart at quarter speed, then run
it whole. This is the single best predictor in Unit 2 of how Checkpoint 2 will go.

### 5. Where the stop makes something new appear — خَوْفٍ

Read 106:4 joined, straight through to the next surah. **خَوْفٍ** is a līn: flat, no
length, exactly as taught in 2-08.

Now stop on it.

It stretches. She will hear it and, if she is paying attention, she will object — because
2-08 told her līn letters have no length.

**She is right, and tell her so.** Then give her the same answer as in 2-12 §3:

> **"Līn has no length. That stretch belongs to the stop, not to the letter. It has a name
> and a count, and Unit 3 gives you both. Today: let it happen and don't measure it."**

**In plain terms:** the stretch is not coming from the letter — it is coming from stopping.
Let it happen and do not try to count it yet.

That is [[Madd-Leen]], met as an observation. Do not name it.

### 6. Recording

Record one reading on a phone. Play it back and have **her** mark the errors before you say
anything.

Self-monitoring is the specific ability the checkpoint's scoring is built around — a
student who self-corrects after a prompt loses one point, one who cannot loses two (see
[[Assessment]] §1) — so it is worth training directly and not as an afterthought. Most
students hear their own dropped ة on a recording instantly, having missed it live.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — you draw the arcs.** Whole surah, pencil, before you read. I check the plan,
then you read it. Every session, a different surah.

**Drill 2 — the metronome.** I tap a slow beat. Stay with it. When you leave the beat, that
word is your weak spot — I will not stop you, but we will come back to it.

**Drill 3 — split attention.** Read Sūrat al-Falaq and stop at every āyah end. While you
read, I will hold up fingers. Tell me the total at the end. Your stops still have to be
correct.

**Drill 4 — the ة gauntlet.** Twelve words ending in ة, non-Qurʾānic, read stopping on each
one: مَدْرَسَة · سَيَّارَة · شَجَرَة · مَدِينَة · جَامِعَة · طَاوِلَة · نَافِذَة ·
حَدِيقَة · سَاعَة · غُرْفَة · مُدَرِّسَة · كَلِمَة. All twelve, no *t* sounds.

**Drill 5 — stop anywhere legal.** I will point at a word. Read to the end of the phrase it
belongs to, stop there properly, then resume. This breaks the habit of only being able to
stop where you rehearsed.

**Drill 6 — record and mark.** Record one surah. Play it back and mark your own errors
before I say anything. Then we compare lists. Anything on my list that is not on yours is
the thing to work on — not the error itself, but the fact that you did not hear it.

**Drill 7 — cold surah.** One surah you have not prepared, once per session, read straight
through. This is the Checkpoint 2 condition in miniature and it should happen weekly, not
once at the end.

## Listen-for

**Listen for:** an even pace with the stops in chosen places, and — the real marker of
fluency — **the same word read the same way twice**, once mid-sentence and once at a stop,
each correct for its position.

- **The mistake:** stops that are correct when isolated but collapse inside a full surah —
  the ة comes back as a *t* on āyah 3 after being right on āyah 1.
  **Why it happens:** the change is being applied consciously, and conscious application
  fails under load. It is a fluency problem masquerading as a knowledge problem, and more
  explaining will not touch it.
  **Say:** *"You know this one. Same surah again, slower — we're building the habit, not
  learning the rule."*

- **The mistake:** the pace surges and drops — fast on ٱللَّهِ and ٱلنَّاسِ, slow on
  ٱلنَّفَّٰثَٰتِ.
  **Why it happens:** familiar words are being recalled and unfamiliar ones decoded. Two
  different processes running in one reading, at two different speeds.
  **Say:** *"Slow the easy words down to the speed of the hard ones. Even, all the way
  through."*

- **The mistake:** stopping in a grammatically incomplete place — after وَمِن شَرِّ, or
  after ٱلَّذِى.
  **Why it happens:** she is stopping where the *breath* ends rather than where the
  *meaning* ends, and at this stage she has no meaning cue to work from.
  **Say:** *"Does that sound finished? Read it again and stop at the end of the āyah — the
  little circle is a safe stop, every time."*

- **The mistake:** a stumble triggers a full restart of the surah.
  **Why it happens:** the surah is stored as one memorised unit, so there is no entry point
  except the beginning. It is a hifz habit leaking into a reading task.
  **Say:** *"Back to the top of that word only. Not the top of the surah. You're reading,
  not reciting."*

- **The mistake:** the six-count madd in ٱلضَّآلِّينَ shortens when it comes at the end of a
  long āyah.
  **Why it happens:** breath. She reached it with nothing left, because the arc before it
  was too long.
  **Say:** *"That's not a madd problem, it's a plan problem. Move your breath mark
  earlier."*

## Homework

1. One surah per day from the hifz set, read with **your own** breath arcs pencilled in
   first. Keep the marked sheets; we compare week to week.
2. Al-Fātiḥa, once daily, with the last āyah taken separately five extra times.
3. Drill 4's twelve ة words, aloud, daily, until no *t* survives.
4. **Record yourself once a week** and mark your own errors before the lesson. Bring both
   the recording and your list.
5. One cold surah a week — one you have not prepared. Read it once, straight through, and
   write down what happened. That reading is not for practice; it is your own early warning
   about Checkpoint 2.
