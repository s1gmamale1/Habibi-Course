# Lesson 3-02 — Ṣifāt with opposites I: hams / jahr, shiddah / rakhāwah

## Objectives

- Student can say what a **ṣifah** is and how it differs from a makhraj — *where* versus *how* — and can explain why two letters at one point need ṣifāt to tell them apart.
- Student can run the **palm test** on any letter and correctly sort it into hams or jahr, and can name the ten hams letters via فَحَثَّهُ شَخْصٌ سَكَتْ.
- Student can hold a rakhāwah letter for three seconds, produce a shiddah letter with a real moment of silence inside it, and place the five tawassut letters between the two.
- Student can say which ṣifah is being described when you say "the *breath*" and which when you say "the *sound*", and does not confuse the two axes.

## Hook

Lead with the error, and make it hers before you name it.

Say **ت** and **د** to her, one after the other, and ask what the difference is. She will say "one is harder" or "they just sound different." Then say: **they are made at exactly the same place.** Same tongue, same tooth roots, same closure. Point 8 on the map she drew last lesson holds ط, د **and** ت. If the makhraj were the whole story, all three would be the same letter.

Then the error itself, which she is almost certainly making right now:

> **ز read as س. ذ read as ث. د read as ت at the end of a word.**

Each of those is one letter sliding into its neighbour at the *same* makhraj, and each of them is a failure of a single ṣifah — the breath. Say يَلِدْ (112:3) and let her hear whether her own د arrives as a د or as a ت. Most students devoice it, because most languages devoice a final stop and nobody has ever told them Arabic does not.

The sentence for the lesson:

> **The makhraj tells you where. The ṣifah tells you how. Two letters can share an address and still be different letters — and the thing that makes them different is what you are about to learn to hear.**

## What they already know

- **Lesson 3-01, last session,** gave her the seventeen points, and it ended on exactly this cliff: the three tooth rows each hold a heavy letter beside its light twin, and the columns طْ صْ ظْ / دْ زْ ذْ / تْ سْ ثْ are organised by something the map cannot show. That something is the ṣifāt. Point back at that table; it is the same table today, read down instead of across.
- **Lesson 1-13** already had her bouncing the five qalqalah letters ق ط ب ج د on a sukūn. She has been doing that for months without being told *why those five*. She will get the answer in lesson 3-05, and it is built out of two of today's ṣifāt. Tell her that now — it makes today's material feel like it is going somewhere.
- **Lesson 2-05** taught her that مّ and نّ hum and that بّ and قّ are held as silence. That is precisely the shiddah / tawassut distinction, learned by ear a year before it had a name.

## Teaching sequence

1. **Define ṣifah in one line, and give the reason it exists.**
   A **ṣifah** (صِفَة, "characteristic", plural *ṣifāt*) is a quality of a letter that is not its place. Ibn al-Jazarī puts the ṣifāt immediately after the makhārij, before any contextual rule — see [[Sifat]] for the full scheme — because a letter's intrinsic properties come before what it becomes in company.
   The practical reason: **ت, د and ط share one makhraj.** What separates them is voicing, heaviness and bounce. Three ṣifāt, three letters.

2. **Show the shape of the whole system once, then close it again.** Five pairs of opposites, and every letter carries exactly one of each pair:

   | Pair | The question it answers |
   |---|---|
   | **hams ↔ jahr** | is the *breath* running or stopped? |
   | **shiddah ↔ rakhāwah** (with **tawassut** between) | is the *sound* blocked or flowing? |
   | isti'lā ↔ istifāl | is the back of the tongue up or down? |
   | iṭbāq ↔ infitāḥ | how much tongue meets the palate? |
   | idhlāq ↔ iṣmāt | how easily does it run off the tongue? |

   **Today is the first two rows only.** Rows three and four are next session and they are the ones that matter most; row five is bookkeeping. Say that, so she knows the map is finite.

3. **Pair 1 — al-hams / al-jahr. This is about BREATH.**

   **Hams** (الهَمْس, "whisper") is the continued flow of breath alongside the letter. The articulators press only lightly, so they never seal the airstream and breath keeps escaping while the letter is being made.

   **Jahr** (الجَهْر, "announcing aloud") is the opposite: the breath is stopped, because the reliance on the makhraj is firm. The sound rides on voice, not on escaping air.

   Ten hams letters, gathered as a sentence:

   > **فَحَثَّهُ شَخْصٌ سَكَتْ** — *fa-ḥaththahu shakhṣun sakat*
   > ف · ح · ث · ه · ش · خ · ص · س · ك · ت

   The remaining nineteen are jahr. Teach the ten and take the rest by subtraction — but tell her the classical mnemonic for the nineteen exists (**عَظُمَ وَزْنُ قَارِئٍ ذِي غَضٍّ جَدَّ طَلَبْ**) so that a book quoting it does not stop her.

   **In plain terms:** hams letters let breath escape with them; jahr letters do not. Put a hand in front of your mouth — if you feel a puff, it is hams.

   **Two physical tests, and you must run both live.**
   - **Palm test.** Hold the palm a few centimetres from the mouth and say the letter with a sukūn. A hams letter puffs on the palm; a jahr letter does not. تْ puffs. دْ does not.
   - **Throat test.** Fingers on the voice box. A jahr letter buzzes through the whole letter. زْ buzzes; سْ does not.

   Then the payoff, said explicitly: **ك and ت are hams even though they are stops.** They block the sound completely and still leak breath on release. That single fact is why they are not qalqalah letters, and she will need it in two lessons' time.

4. **Pair 2 — ash-shiddah / ar-rakhāwah, with at-tawassut in the middle. This is about SOUND.**

   Do not let her merge this with pair 1. Say it as a slogan and make her repeat it:

   > **Hams and jahr are about the BREATH. Shiddah and rakhāwah are about the SOUND. A letter has one of each.**

   **Shiddah** (الشِّدَّة, "strength") — the sound is **completely blocked** at the makhraj, then released. There is a real moment of silence inside the letter. Eight letters:

   > **أَجِدْ قَطٍ بَكَتْ** — *ajid qaṭin bakat*
   > ء · ج · د · ق · ط · ب · ك · ت

   **Rakhāwah** (الرَّخاوة, "softness") — the sound **flows continuously**. The channel narrows but never seals, so the letter can be held as long as the breath lasts. Sixteen letters, reached by subtraction: ث ح خ ذ ز س ش ص ض ظ غ ف ه و ي ا.

   **Tawassut** (التَّوَسُّط, "betweenness"; also *al-bayniyyah*, البينية) — **neither**. The airstream is obstructed at the makhraj but escapes by another route: around the sides of the tongue (ل), through the nose (ن, م), by deviation (ر), or through a narrowed but voiced throat (ع). Five letters:

   > **لِنْ عُمَرْ** — *lin ʿumar*
   > ل · ن · ع · م · ر

   Say plainly that tawassut has **no opposite of its own**: it is the midpoint of one scale, not an eleventh ṣifah with a partner. Some books present it as having opposites on both sides; the sounds described are identical either way.

5. **Teach the scale by the holding test, not by the list.** One question, asked out loud, for every letter:

   > **Can you hold it? And if you can, does it hiss or does it ring?**

   - Cannot hold it at all — there is silence inside — **shiddah**.
   - Can hold it, and it hisses or rushes — **rakhāwah**.
   - Can hold it, and it *rings* rather than hisses — **tawassut**.

   Run it live on قْ (cannot), سْ (hisses), لْ (rings). Three letters, three answers, thirty seconds. She now owns the scale.

6. **Now cross the two axes, because this is the step everyone skips.** Build this grid on the board with her:

   | | hams (breath runs) | jahr (breath stops) |
   |---|---|---|
   | **shiddah** | **ك · ت** | ء · ج · د · ق · ط · ب |
   | **tawassut** | — | ل · ن · ع · م · ر |
   | **rakhāwah** | ف ح ث ه ش خ ص س | ذ ز ض ظ غ و ي ا |

   Two things fall straight out of the grid, and both are load-bearing:
   - The **shiddah + jahr** cell has six letters. Take out the hamzah (excluded by transmission) and you have **ق ط ب ج د** — the qalqalah five, exactly. She has been bouncing them since 1-13. In lesson 3-05 she will be able to *derive* them.
   - The **shiddah + hams** cell has ك and ت, and that cell is why those two do not bounce.

   Say the conclusion out loud: **the ṣifāt compose. They are not a list of labels stuck on letters; they are properties that combine and produce behaviour.**

7. **Close on ع, which is where the tawassut damage lives.**
   ع is the most-cited error for English, Turkish, Uzbek, Persian and Urdu speakers, and it fails in exactly two directions:
   - **ع as a glottal stop** — a hard catch in the throat. That is a ء. That is shiddah, not tawassut.
   - **ع as an open vowel** — a strained "aa" with nothing in it.
   A correct ع is **squeezed at the middle of the throat and audible while it is being held.** She has had this since 1-13; today it gets its name and its test. Hold ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ (1:2) and count three seconds on the ع.

## Examples

| Text | Ref | What it demonstrates |
|---|---|---|
| ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ | 1:6 | س and ت — hams. The breath must keep running through both |
| فَصَلِّ | 108:2 | ف and ص — hams, two puffs on the palm in one short word |
| ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ | 1:2 | jahr throughout, and four tawassut letters: ع, ل, م, ن |
| قُلْ | 112:1 | shiddah ق — block, silence, release; then a tawassut ل |
| تَبَّتْ | 111:1 | ت shiddah + hams, ب shiddah + jahr — the same axis, opposite values |
| غَاسِقٍ | 113:3 | rakhāwah — غ and س can each be held for three seconds |
| أَنْعَمْتَ | 1:7 | ن, ع, م — three of the five tawassut letters, in a row |

**Teacher note on تَبَّتْ.** This is the sharpest single word for pair 1. The ت and the ب are both shiddah — both fully blocked — yet the ت puffs on the palm and the ب does not. Same sound-behaviour, opposite breath-behaviour. Have her say *tab-bat* with her palm up and count the puffs: two, on the first ت and the final ت, and nothing on the ب.

**Teacher note on ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ.** Four of the five لِنْ عُمَرْ letters appear here — ع, ل, م, ن — which makes it the natural tawassut drill word and, not coincidentally, the word where a weak ع is most audible in her daily prayer.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — the palm.** Palm up, a few centimetres from the mouth. Say each letter closed by a sukūn and call out "puff" or "no puff" *before* you name the ṣifah:

تْ · دْ · سْ · زْ · ثْ · ذْ · صْ · ضْ · كْ · قْ · فْ · بْ · شْ · جْ · هْ · ءْ

The pairs are deliberate. Each one is a same-makhraj neighbour whose only difference is the breath.

**Drill 2 — the mnemonic as language.** Say the sentence, do not spell it:

> **فَحَثَّهُ شَخْصٌ سَكَتْ**

Say it five times as a sentence, then extract the ten letters from it out loud. It means *"a person who had fallen silent urged him on"* — memorise the sentence and the list comes free.

**Drill 3 — the holding test.** Three seconds each, out loud, on a stopwatch:

| Hold it | Cannot hold it |
|---|---|
| صْ · شْ · سْ · ذْ · ظْ · فْ · حْ | قْ · طْ · بْ · جْ · دْ · كْ · تْ · ءْ |

If a letter in the left column dies before three seconds, it is being made as a stop. If one in the right column can be held, it is leaking.

**Drill 4 — the ringing five.** لِنْ عُمَرْ. Hold each for three seconds and check that it *rings* rather than hisses:

لْ · نْ · عْ · مْ · رْ

Then pinch the nose on نْ and مْ only. Those two should die; the other three should be untouched.

**Drill 5 — the ع, on its own, because it needs it.** Three seconds, squeezed, voiced, no catch and no plain vowel:

أَعْ · عَا · نَعْ · ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ · أَنْعَمْتَ · فَلْيَعْبُدُوا۟

If it starts with a click, it has become a hamzah. If it has no squeeze, it has become a vowel.

**Drill 6 — devoicing patrol.** These are the five same-makhraj slides that ruin words. Say each pair, then the Qur'anic word, and check the second member is not turning into the first:

| Should be | Do not let it become | Test word |
|---|---|---|
| ز | س | ٱلْجِنَّةِ ... (any word-final voiced stop) |
| ذ | ث | — |
| د | ت | يَلِدْ (112:3) |
| ط | ت | أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ (108:1) |
| ض | ص | ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ (1:7) |

Fingers on the throat for the whole drill. If the buzz stops before the letter does, it has devoiced.

**Drill 7 — two axes, cold.** For each letter, give **both** answers in order — breath first, sound second: "ت: hams, shiddah." No hesitating:

ت · ز · ل · ص · ق · م · خ · ب · ن · ش · د · ع · ف · ط

## Listen-for

**1. Devoicing a jahr letter at the end of a word.**
- *Listen for:* يَلِدْ ending in a ت. Fingers on the throat settle it in one second — the buzz should last through the د.
- *Why it happens:* Uzbek, Turkish, German, Russian and many others devoice final stops as a rule of the language. She is applying her own phonology, correctly, to the wrong language.
- *Correction cue:* "Keep the buzz on to the very end. Hand on your throat — I want to feel it stop *after* the letter, not before."

**2. Losing hams on word-final ت and ك.**
- *Listen for:* a clipped, swallowed ending on تَبَّتْ or أَعْطَيْنَٰكَ, with no air on the palm.
- *Why it happens:* she is concentrating on stopping cleanly, and a clean stop feels like it should be silent. Hams says otherwise: the breath leaks *on the release*.
- *Correction cue:* "Palm up. Say it again. I want to feel that."

**3. ص collapsing toward a bare س stop.**
- *Listen for:* a ص that has heaviness but no flow — it starts and stops instead of hissing.
- *Why it happens:* the student clamps down while reaching for heaviness and turns a fricative into a plosive. The heaviness is right; the continuity is missing.
- *Correction cue:* "Hold it. Three seconds. Heavy *and* running." Full treatment in 3-03.

**4. ج leaking into a continuous "zh".**
- *Listen for:* a ج you can hold. You should not be able to — it is shiddah, and there must be a real silence inside it.
- *Why it happens:* the nearest sound in most students' phonology is a fricative, and the fricative is easier.
- *Correction cue:* "Close it completely. Nothing comes out. Now let it go." Test on يَجْعَلْ (105:2).

**5. ع produced as a hamzah, or as nothing.**
- *Listen for:* a click at the start of the ع, or a strained open vowel with no squeeze in it.
- *Why it happens:* neither sound exists in her first language, and both failures are *plausible* substitutions that native listeners will still parse.
- *Correction cue:* "Squeeze the middle of your throat and keep it running. It should be holdable — if it stops instantly you made a hamzah; if it has no squeeze you made a vowel."

**6. Answering the wrong axis.**
- *Listen for:* "ت — rakhāwah?" or "س — jahr?" She is not confused about the letters; she is confused about which question she was asked.
- *Why it happens:* both pairs are taught in one lesson and both sound like "hard versus soft" in English.
- *Correction cue:* "Breath or sound? Which one am I asking about?" Then re-ask. Drill 7 exists for this and should be run at the top of every session until it is automatic.

**7. Bouncing a tawassut letter.**
- *Listen for:* a sākin ر or ل released with a kick, as though it were a qalqalah letter.
- *Why it happens:* a sākin ر genuinely feels bouncy, and she has been bouncing five letters since 1-13.
- *Correction cue:* "Only blocked letters bounce. ر is not blocked — you can hold it. Hold it." Test on وَٱنْحَرْ (108:2) and ٱلْفَلَقِ's ل.

## Hifz for this session

**Manzil / Sabqi first.** 1:1–1:2 from last session, from memory, before anything new. Ten extra repetitions on any word you have to correct — as its own step, not folded into the general count.

**New Sabaq — al-Fātiḥa 1:3–1:4.**

> ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ
> مَٰلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ

Two short ayat, twenty to forty repetitions, read from the page. Two tajweed points to hold her to while she memorises, both from today:

- The **ح** of ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ and ٱلرَّحِيمِ is hams and rakhāwah — breath running, sound running, held at the middle of the throat. It must not arrive as a soft ه or as a scraped خ.
- The **د** of ٱلدِّينِ is jahr and shiddah — voiced right through, with a real closure. Doubled here, so it is held; the buzz must not drop out in the middle of the hold.

## Homework

**20–30 min/day, five days.**

1. **Two minutes — Drill 1, the palm.** Sixteen letters, "puff" or "no puff" out loud before naming anything.
2. **One minute — Drill 2.** Say فَحَثَّهُ شَخْصٌ سَكَتْ five times as a sentence, then list the ten letters from it.
3. **Three minutes — Drill 3, the holding test**, on a stopwatch. Any left-column letter that dies before three seconds gets ten more repetitions.
4. **Two minutes — Drill 5, the ع.** Every day. This one does not get skipped.
5. **Two minutes — Drill 6, devoicing patrol,** fingers on the throat.
6. **Two minutes — Drill 7, two axes cold.** Breath first, sound second, no pauses.
7. **Hifz — Manzil/Sabqi (1:1–1:2), then new Sabaq (1:3–1:4).** Revision before new material, every single day.

**Bring to the next session:** one letter where the palm test and your instinct disagreed, and a recording of yourself saying تَبَّتْ. We will count the puffs together.
