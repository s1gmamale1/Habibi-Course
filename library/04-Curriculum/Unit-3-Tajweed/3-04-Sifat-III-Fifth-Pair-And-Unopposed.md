# Lesson 3-04 — Ṣifāt III: the fifth pair, and the ṣifāt with no opposite

> **This lesson closes the ṣifāt.** Lessons 3-02 and 3-03 taught four of the five opposite pairs. This one teaches the fifth, and then the characteristics that have no opposite at all. Two of them — qalqalah and ghunnah — are the machinery behind roughly half the rules in the rest of the course, and both are taught here as *properties of letters* before they return as *rules*.

## Objectives

- Student can name the fifth pair, **idhlāq / iṣmāt**, recite **فِرَّ مِنْ لُبِّ**, and say why this pair — unlike the other four — changes nothing about how she pronounces anything.
- Student can produce **ر with exactly one contact** and can hear when she has produced two.
- Student can name the ṣifāt with no opposite and point to the letter or letters each one belongs to.
- Student can say why exactly **five** letters have qalqalah and why exactly **two** carry ghunnah, deriving both from ṣifāt she already knows rather than memorising the lists.

## Hook

Ask her to say **ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ** (1:3), and to say it the way she always says it.

Then ask one question: **how many times did your tongue touch?**

She will not know, because nobody has ever asked. So have her say it again with a finger resting lightly under her chin, slowly, and count. If she comes from Uzbek, Russian, Persian, Urdu, Spanish or Italian, the answer will be **two or three**. She has been rolling the ر of the Divine Name every day of her life and has never once heard it.

Then say the sentence the lesson turns on:

> **Everything you have learned so far in the ṣifāt is something to produce. The next one is the only characteristic in the whole system that exists so you can avoid it.**

Then the second hook, which sets up the back half. Write **قُطْبُ جَدٍّ** on the board and ask: *why these five letters?* Let her guess. She will guess that they are simply a list to memorise. They are not — she already knows both ṣifāt that produce them, from lesson 3-02, and by the end of the session she will derive the five herself.

## What they already know

- **Lesson 3-02** gave her the first two pairs — hams/jahr and shiddah/tawassuṭ/rakhāwah. **Today those two pairs come back as the cause of something.** Qalqalah is not a sixth thing to memorise; it is what happens when shiddah and jahr occur on the same sākin letter. She has had the ingredients since 3-02 without being told what they make.
- **Lesson 3-03** gave her isti'lā'/istifāl and iṭbāq/infitāḥ, and with them the habit of thinking in *sets of letters with a mnemonic*. Today adds two more mnemonics — **فِرَّ مِنْ لُبِّ** and **قُطْبُ جَدٍّ** — and one of them she will keep for the rest of the course.
- **Lesson 3-01** placed ر and ل next to each other on the tongue map and noted that they are the two letters whose sound "goes around" the contact rather than through it. Today names that: **inḥirāf**.
- **Lesson 1-03** taught her س, ش and ز as a family by sound. Today explains what actually separates them, and it is not what she thinks.

## Teaching sequence

### 1. Where this lesson sits — draw the whole ṣifāt system once

Before any new content, put the map on one sheet. She has seen pieces; she has not seen the shape.

| | Pairs | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | hams / jahr | 3-02 ✓ |
| 2 | shiddah / tawassuṭ / rakhāwah | 3-02 ✓ |
| 3 | isti'lā' / istifāl | 3-03 ✓ |
| 4 | iṭbāq / infitāḥ | 3-03 ✓ |
| 5 | **idhlāq / iṣmāt** | **today** |
| | **With no opposite** | **today** |
| | ṣafīr · inḥirāf · takrīr · tafashshī · istiṭālah · leen · qalqalah · ghunnah | |

Say the count out loud: **every letter in the alphabet carries exactly one member of each of the five pairs — five ṣifāt each, no exceptions.** The unopposed ones are different in kind: a letter either has one or the description simply does not apply.

**In plain terms:** the five pairs are like five questions you ask about every single letter, and every letter answers all five. The unopposed ones are extras that only a few letters have at all.

### 2. Pair 5 — al-idhlāq / al-iṣmāt

**Idhlāq** (الإذْلاق) is *sharpness and fluency of speech* — a *dhaliq al-lisān* is someone with a quick, ready tongue. The word also names the **tip or edge of the tongue** (*dhalq*). Technically it is the **ease and speed with which a letter is pronounced**, and the reason is anatomical: these letters come either from the edge of the tongue (ر, ن, ل) or from the lips (ف, م, ب) — the two most mobile and least effortful articulators in the mouth.

Six letters, in one mnemonic:

> # فِرَّ مِنْ لُبِّ
> *firra min lubb*
> **ف · ر · م · ن · ل · ب**

Teach the split at the same time as the phrase, because the split is the actual reason for the grouping: **three from the tongue's edge (ر ن ل), three from the lips (ف م ب).**

**Iṣmāt** (الإصْمات) is *preventing, holding back* — the remaining twenty-three letters, whose makhārij lie away from the edge and the lips. There is no positive mnemonic; the set is always reached by subtracting the six.

Now the honest part, and say it plainly rather than hiding it: **this pair has almost no effect on how you recite.** Its classical justification is lexical, not acoustic. A native Arabic root of four or five letters always contains at least one idhlāq letter — a long word made only of iṣmāt letters is a sign the word is borrowed. That is a fact about the dictionary, not about her mouth.

- **114:1** بِرَبِّ ٱلنَّاسِ — ب, ر and ن are all idhlāq; the word runs off the tongue
- **113:5** حَاسِدٍ — ح ا س د, not one idhlāq letter in it, and it is noticeably more work to say
- **1:2** ٱلْحَمْدُ — ل and م idhlāq, ح and د iṣmāt, in one short word

**In plain terms:** six letters are the easy ones because your lips and tongue-tip do the work. The other twenty-three are further back and cost more effort. Knowing which is which will not change a single sound you make — it completes the system, and it tells you where you will get tired.

### 3. Takrīr — the one ṣifah you learn in order **not** to do it

**Takrīr** (التَّكْرير, "repetition") is the **tendency of the tongue tip to vibrate repeatedly** against the gum ridge while ر is produced. The tip is held loosely, and the airstream flaps it more than once. One letter only: **ر**.

Every other characteristic in the ṣifāt is something the student must *produce*. Takrīr is the exception, and the classical formulation is explicit: **the ṣifah is named so that it may be guarded against.**

> **A correct ر has exactly one contact.** The tip strikes the ridge once and releases. Two or more strikes is a trill, and a trill multiplies the letter — one ر heard as several.

Name whose problem this is, and be specific, because it changes how you correct it. Uzbek, Russian, Persian, Urdu, Spanish and Italian all have a genuinely rolled r. **The student is producing a sound that is correct in her own language.** She is not being careless, and telling her to "try harder" will do nothing. This is not a beginner's error that fades with practice; unchecked it hardens and survives for years.

The opposite failure is the English approximant — a ر with *no* contact at all, the tongue merely bunching. That one is a missing contact rather than a doubled one, and the fix is the reverse.

- **1:3** ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ — the highest-frequency ر in her life, and a shaddah, which doubles the temptation
- **114:1** بِرَبِّ ٱلنَّاسِ — a shaddah'd ر again, in the ayah she says constantly
- **105:4** بِحِجَارَةٍ — a ر with a plain fatḥah, easier to control; use it to establish the target first

**The correction is mechanical, not auditory.** Have her press the tip firmly and deliberately, once, and *stop*. A firmly held tip cannot flap; a loose one will. On a shaddah'd ر the hold is longer, not the number of taps.

**In plain terms:** tap once. If your tongue flutters like a little motor, that is one ر turning into three. Press harder and it stops.

### 4. Inḥirāf — the sound that bends around the contact

**Inḥirāf** (الانْحِراف, "turning aside") is the **deviation of the sound away from its own makhraj toward another point**. The tongue makes contact, the airstream cannot pass through that contact, and the sound bends around it instead of stopping.

Two letters, deviating in opposite directions:

- **ل** — the tip touches the gum ridge, and the sound deviates **around the sides** of the tongue
- **ر** — the tip touches slightly further back, and the sound deviates **toward the back**

Both are tawassuṭ letters from 3-02, and inḥirāf is the *mechanism behind* that tawassuṭ: the sound is neither blocked nor freely flowing precisely because it is detouring.

The test is a **held sound that still has a point of contact**. Ask for a sustained sākin ل: the tip must stay planted for the whole duration while the sound continues around it. If the tip lifts, the letter becomes a vowel. If the sound stops, it becomes a د.

- **1:2** ٱلْحَمْدُ — a sākin ل, holdable, with the tip planted
- **112:3** يُولَدْ — ل then a sākin د; the first bends, the second stops dead

**In plain terms:** for ل and ر your tongue blocks the middle, so the sound goes around it instead of through. That is why you can hold them — and if you can't hold it, your tongue has come off the roof.

### 5. Ṣafīr — the whistle

**Ṣafīr** (الصَّفير) is the sound made to call a bird or a horse. Technically it is a **sharp, whistle-like sound emerging alongside the letter**, produced by the very narrow channel between the tongue tip and the back of the lower front teeth. The air is forced through a slit rather than an opening, and the resulting high-frequency hiss **is part of the letter, not an artefact of it.**

Three letters — **ص · ز · س** — and they differ from each other by their *other* ṣifāt, not by the whistle:

| Letter | Breath | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| س | hams | istifāl — light |
| ز | jahr | istifāl — light |
| ص | hams | isti'lā' + iṭbāq — heavy |

This is worth dwelling on, because it is the cleanest demonstration in the whole unit that **ṣifāt stack**. Three letters, one shared whistle, told apart entirely by characteristics she learned in the last two lessons.

- **1:6** ٱلصِّرَٰطَ — a ص that is heavy *and* whistling; the heaviness does not cancel the whistle
- **114:1** ٱلنَّاسِ — a plain light س, the reference whistle

**In plain terms:** س, ز and ص all hiss. What makes them different is everything else — whether your voice is on, and whether the back of your tongue is up.

### 6. Tafashshī — the breath spread wide

**Tafashshī** (التَّفَشِّي) is *spreading out, diffusing* — the word is used of news that gets around. Technically it is the **spreading of the breath widely inside the mouth**: the air is not channelled through a narrow slit as in the ṣafīr letters but released across the full width of the tongue and palate, filling the mouth before it leaves.

One letter: **ش**.

Tafashshī is exactly what distinguishes ش from س. Both are hams (breath flows) and rakhāwah (sound flows) and istifāl (light). **The only difference is whether the breath is concentrated into a slit or spread across the mouth.**

- **س** — narrow, high, focused: a whistle
- **ش** — wide, low, spread: a rush of air

The characteristic error is the **narrow English "sh"** as in *ship*, made with rounded lips and a tightly grooved tongue. It is recognisably in the right family, which is why it survives correction for so long. Unround the lips and let the tongue go flat and wide.

- **106:1** قُرَيْشٍ — a ش at the end of a word she already has memorised

**In plain terms:** for س the air goes through a straw. For ش it fills your whole mouth first. Same breath, different width.

### 7. Qalqalah as a ṣifah — and now derive the five letters

Do not give her the list. Make her build it.

**Qalqalah** (القَلْقَلة) is *disturbance, shaking*. Technically it is a **disturbance or echo in the makhraj when the letter is sākin**, heard as a small bounce at the moment of release.

It is not an extra property bolted onto five arbitrary letters. It is the **necessary consequence of two ṣifāt she already knows occurring together**:

- **shiddah** — the sound is completely blocked, so it cannot flow out
- **jahr** — the breath is stopped, so it cannot leak out either

A sākin letter that is both blocked *and* voiced has nowhere to go. When the articulators part, the trapped sound escapes as an audible bounce.

Now walk the exclusions with her, out loud, because this is where the derivation becomes hers:

> Of the eight **shiddah** letters, ك and ت are **hams** — the breath escapes, so nothing is trapped and there is no bounce. Hamzah is shiddah and jahr, but it is excluded by transmission: it is released as a clean glottal stop. **What remains is five.**

> # قُطْبُ جَدٍّ
> *quṭbu jaddin* — **ق · ط · ب · ج · د**

- **105:4** بِحِجَارَةٍ — a sākin ج mid-word; the easiest bounce to hear
- **112:3** يُولَدْ — a sākin د at a stop
- **111:1** وَتَبَّ — a ب at a stop carrying a shaddah, the largest bounce in the short surahs

**Refuse the rule today, and say that you are refusing it.** The *sizes* of the bounce — small mid-word, large at a stop — are the next two lessons, and they have names and numbers she will get then. Today the question is only *why these five letters and not others*.

**In plain terms:** these five get stuck. Your mouth closes completely and your voice is switched on, so the sound is trapped with nowhere to go — and when you open, it pops out. That pop is qalqalah.

### 8. Ghunnah as a ṣifah — the sound that leaves through the nose

**Ghunnah** (الغُنَّة) is a nasal twang in the voice. Technically it is a **nasal resonance issuing from the khayshum** (الخَيْشُوم — the nasal passage), and it is **inseparable** from ن and م. It is not added to these two letters in certain positions; it is part of what they are. Their consonantal makhraj is in the mouth — the tongue tip for ن, the two lips for م — but the *sound* comes out through the nose.

Because the khayshum is the source, **ghunnah is the only ṣifah whose makhraj lies outside the mouth and throat entirely.**

Every ن and every م carries ghunnah in every position. What changes from position to position is not *whether* it is present but **how complete it is** — and that gradient is the whole of Units 3.3 and 3.4.

**The pinch test.** Have her hold the نّ of ٱلنَّاسِ and pinch her nostrils shut. If the sound stops dead, the ghunnah is genuine. If it continues unchanged, she is making an oral "n".

- **114:1** بِرَبِّ ٱلنَّاسِ — a نّ with a shaddah, the most complete ghunnah there is
- **1:2** ٱلْحَمْدُ — a sākin م with ghunnah present but not prolonged

**Refuse the rule here too.** She does not get lengths today. The two-count hold, and every rule built on it, is lesson 3-15 onward.

**In plain terms:** ن and م always hum through your nose — that is just what they are. Pinch your nose and the sound should die. Later you will learn how long to hold the hum; today only that it is always there.

### 9. Point forward, explicitly

- **Qalqalah** returns as two rules in **3-05** and **3-06** — the small bounce and the large one. Today's derivation is the reason those two lessons need no new letter list.
- **Ghunnah** returns as a timed rule in **3-15**, and then every single rule in Units 3.3 and 3.4 is built on it.
- **Takrīr** is checked in every lesson from here on. It is the one item from today that goes into the daily warm-up permanently.
- **Istiṭālah**, the unopposed ṣifah belonging to ض alone, gets its own lesson at **3-08** because it is the hardest letter in the alphabet for her language background.
- **Leen** she already met in **2-08**; it returns inside the madd family at **3-31**.

**Teacher note on ٱلْحَمْدُ.** This one word carries three of today's ṣifāt in sequence — a bending ل, a humming م, a bouncing sākin د — and she says it every day. If she can name all three in it by the end of the session, the lesson has landed.

**Counter-example, and use it deliberately.** ٱلصِّرَٰطَ *looks* like it should have a qalqalah on the ط. It does not, because the ط here is not sākin. Qalqalah is a property of the letter **when sākin**; a vowelled ق ط ب ج د does not bounce. Have her check the ط, expect it to bounce, and find that it cannot.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — the tap count.** Say ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ (1:3) five times, slowly, counting the contacts of your tongue tip each time. The answer must be one contact per ر, every time. If you cannot tell, press the tip harder — a firm tip cannot flutter.

**Drill 2 — the two mnemonics.** Say each phrase three times, then name its letters, then say the phrase again:

> **فِرَّ مِنْ لُبِّ** — ف · ر · م · ن · ل · ب — the six easy ones
> **قُطْبُ جَدٍّ** — ق · ط · ب · ج · د — the five that bounce

**Drill 3 — derive the five, cold.** Do not recite قُطْبُ جَدٍّ. Instead answer out loud: *which letters are both shiddah and jahr?* Then: *which of those are excluded, and why?* You should arrive at five letters without using the mnemonic. Do this until the derivation is faster than the recall.

**Drill 4 — the whistle set.** Say each letter held for a full second: سْ · زْ · صْ. All three whistle. Then say what separates each from the others in two words — "breath, light" / "voice, light" / "breath, heavy".

**Drill 5 — س against ش.** Say سْ then شْ, back to back, four times. On the س the air should feel like it is going through a straw. On the ش it should fill your whole mouth. If they feel the same width, the ش is still an English "sh" — unround your lips and flatten your tongue.

**Drill 6 — the hold test for ل and ر.** Say a sākin ل and hold it for three full seconds with your tongue tip planted and not moving. Then the same on ر — but one contact only, so hold the *position*, not a flutter. If the sound stops, your tongue has sealed too hard; if it turns into a vowel, the tip has come off.

**Drill 7 — the pinch test.** Say ٱلنَّاسِ (114:1) and, while holding the نّ, pinch your nose shut. The sound must stop dead. Repeat on the م of ٱلْحَمْدُ (1:2).

**Drill 8 — bounce or no bounce.** Say each and answer "bounce" or "no bounce", no hesitation:

يُولَدْ (112:3) · ٱلْحَمْدُ (1:2) · بِحِجَارَةٍ (105:4) · ٱلصِّرَٰطَ (1:6) · وَتَبَّ (111:1)

The trap is ٱلصِّرَٰطَ. The ط is a qalqalah letter but it is not sākin, so there is nothing to bounce.

## Listen-for

**1. A rolled ر — two or more contacts.**
- *Listen for:* a flutter on ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ, بِرَبِّ, ٱلصِّرَٰطَ. Easiest to catch on a shaddah'd ر, where the hold gives the tongue time to flap.
- *Why it happens:* her first language has a genuinely rolled r. **She is producing a sound that is correct in her own language**, so nothing feels wrong to her, and she has no internal signal that anything needs changing. Say this to her — it removes the sense that she is being careless.
- *Correction cue:* "Press the tip firmly and stop. One touch." Mechanical, not auditory — she cannot yet hear the difference she is being asked to make.

**2. A ر with no contact at all.**
- *Listen for:* an English *r*, the tongue bunching without touching anything. The mirror error, and common in students who have over-corrected away from a roll.
- *Why it happens:* over-correction, or transfer from English. Both produce the same missing contact.
- *Correction cue:* "Your tongue has to touch. One touch, but a real one." Have her exaggerate to a single hard tap, then relax it.

**3. A narrow, English ش.**
- *Listen for:* rounded lips and a tight groove on قُرَيْشٍ. It sounds close enough to survive correction for months.
- *Why it happens:* English *sh* is a real and nearly-right sound, so nothing signals an error.
- *Correction cue:* "Unround your lips and let your tongue go flat and wide. The air should fill your mouth, not go through a slot."

**4. The whistle disappearing on a heavy ص.**
- *Listen for:* ٱلصِّرَٰطَ where the ص has gone dark but the hiss has gone with it.
- *Why it happens:* she is spending all her attention on the heaviness she learned last lesson, and the whistle is the thing that drops.
- *Correction cue:* "Say س. Now keep exactly that hiss and raise the back of your tongue." Build it from the light letter, not from scratch.

**5. A ل or ر whose tip lifts during the hold.**
- *Listen for:* a sustained ل that turns into a vowel partway through.
- *Why it happens:* she is treating them as flowing letters and forgetting they still have a contact — inḥirāf means the sound goes *around* the contact, not that the contact is optional.
- *Correction cue:* "Keep the tip planted the whole time. The sound goes around it — the tongue does not move."

**6. Bouncing a vowelled qalqalah letter.**
- *Listen for:* a small *uh* added after a ق ط ب ج د that already carries a vowel — often right after this lesson, as an over-application.
- *Why it happens:* she has just learned that five letters bounce and has not yet attached the condition **when sākin**.
- *Correction cue:* "Does it have a vowel? Then it is already moving, and there is nothing trapped to pop." Then Drill 8, including the ٱلصِّرَٰطَ trap.

**7. Adding a full vowel to the bounce.**
- *Listen for:* يُولَدْ becoming *yūlada*.
- *Why it happens:* the bounce needs *some* sound, and the nearest available sound is a vowel she already knows.
- *Correction cue:* "It is a knock, not a syllable. Shorter than the shortest vowel you can say." Full treatment in 3-05.

**8. An oral "n" or "m" that fails the pinch test.**
- *Listen for:* ٱلنَّاسِ where pinching the nose changes nothing.
- *Why it happens:* many languages nasalise much more weakly than Arabic, so her default *n* is largely oral.
- *Correction cue:* the pinch test itself is the correction — it gives her the feedback signal she is missing. Have her find the buzz with the nose shut, then open.

## Hifz for this session

**Revision only. No new Sabaq.** Al-Fātiḥa 1:1–1:6 is complete as of last lesson; this session consolidates rather than adding.

Read 1:1–1:6 from memory, and on each pass listen for exactly one thing from today:

1. Pass one — **every ر.** ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ (1:3), ٱلرَّحِيمِ (1:3), ٱلصِّرَٰطَ (1:6). One contact each.
2. Pass two — **every ن and م.** Are they humming, or are they oral? Pinch-test any that sound thin.
3. Pass three — **ٱلْحَمْدُ** on its own, naming the ل, the م and the د as she says them.

Three passes on six ayat she already holds is worth more today than a new ayah, because every one of today's ṣifāt is already inside them.

## Homework

**15–20 min/day, five days.**

1. **Two minutes — Drill 1, the tap count.** ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ five times, counting contacts. This is the item that matters most; do it first, every day.
2. **Two minutes — Drill 2, the two mnemonics.** فِرَّ مِنْ لُبِّ and قُطْبُ جَدٍّ, three times each, then the letters.
3. **Three minutes — Drill 3, derive the five.** Shiddah and jahr, then the exclusions. Faster than recall by day five.
4. **Two minutes — Drill 5, س against ش.** Four pairs, checking the width each time.
5. **Two minutes — Drill 6, the hold test.** Sākin ل and sākin ر, three seconds each, tip planted.
6. **One minute — Drill 7, the pinch test.** ٱلنَّاسِ and ٱلْحَمْدُ.
7. **Three minutes — Drill 8, bounce or no bounce**, including the ٱلصِّرَٰطَ trap.
8. **Hifz — 1:1–1:6 from memory, three passes**, listening for one thing per pass as above.

**Bring to the next session:** both mnemonics from memory, the derivation of قُطْبُ جَدٍّ without reciting it, and a recording of yourself reading al-Fātiḥa 1:1–1:6. We will count your ر contacts together.
