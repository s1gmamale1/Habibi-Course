# Lesson 4.1 — Kalima Ṭayyiba

> **Why this note is `needs-review`.** The kalimas are not Qurʾān. Their wording is a
> traditional formula, not a revealed text, so it cannot be checked against the pinned
> corpus the way every other Arabic string in this library is. The Arabic below is
> therefore in the **body**, never in `examples:` — the validator would (correctly)
> reject it. Before this lesson is taught, **check the wording letter by letter against a
> printed Hanafi primer** and change the status to `verified` in the
> [[Verification-Log]]. The *tajweed analysis* in this note is sound either way; it is
> the *text* that needs a human eye.

## Why this unit exists at all

Say this to yourself before you say anything to the student.

The six kalimas, together with Īmān Mujmal and Īmān Mufaṣṣal, are the standard
memorisation set of the Hanafi and Central Asian tradition. **They are not tajweed rules
and they are not Qurʾān.** They are creedal formulae. No Arabic-language course and no
typical English tajweed syllabus contains them.

They are in this course for one honest reason: the owner of this course learned tajweed
from an Uzbek course ([[Muallimi-Soniy]]) whose lessons 70–77 are exactly this, and he
chose to keep them.

**This unit is optional relative to the four end goals of the course** — reading an
unseen muṣḥaf page cleanly, reciting the memorised surahs, naming and explaining the
rules, and reading a colour-coded muṣḥaf. Nothing in Unit 4 is tested by any of those
four. Skipping it costs the student nothing on the final checkpoint (see [[Assessment]]).

What it *does* give is a genuinely good thing, and it is worth naming: **Arabic the
student already recites daily, on which every rule from Unit 3 can be applied, where a
mistake is not a mistake in the recitation of the Qurʾān.** That is a rare practice
surface. Treat Unit 4 as applied tajweed with the stakes turned down, and it earns its
place.

## Objectives

- Student can recite Kalima Ṭayyiba from memory with correct tajweed, in one breath.
- Student can **name** the rules occurring in it, pointing at where each one lands.
- Student can state the [[Lam-Jalalah]] rule and apply it to both occurrences here.
- Student can produce [[Idgham-Bila-Ghunnah]] at *muḥammadun rasūl* without a nūn sound.

## Hook

Do not open with the text. Open with a question:

> **"How many times have you said this?"**

Let her answer. Whatever number she gives is too low. Then:

> **"And have you ever once applied a tajweed rule to it?"**

That is the hook of the entire unit, and it only works if you let the silence sit for a
second. She has recited this sentence since childhood — before she could read, before
she knew a makhraj from a ṣifah — and it has never been *corrected*. It has been
**repeated**, which is a different thing and which makes errors permanent rather than
fixing them.

Say it plainly:

> **"You already know these words. This unit is not about learning them. It is about
> saying them properly for the first time."**

## The text

**لَا إِلٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ مُحَمَّدٌ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ**

*lā ilāha illa-llāhu muḥammadur rasūlu-llāh*

> "There is no god except Allah; Muḥammad is the Messenger of Allah."

**Ṭayyiba** (طَيِّبَة) means *pure*, *wholesome*, *good* — the Word of Purity. It is the
shortest of the eight and the foundation of the other five kalimas: **every one of them
either contains it or extends it.** Teach it well and you have taught a third of the
unit.

## Rules occurring in it

Walk the sentence left to right — that is, right to left on the page — and stop at each
rule. This walk is the actual content of the lesson; the memorisation is already done.

### 1. لَا إِلٰهَ — [[Madd-Munfasil]]

The very first two words contain the rule most students are most surprised to find here.

**لَا** ends in a madd letter (fatḥa + alif). **إِلٰهَ** begins with a hamzah. A madd
letter at the end of one word, a hamzah at the start of the next, *separated* by the word
boundary — that is **madd munfaṣil**, the "separated madd", and in the recitation of Ḥafṣ
it is stretched to **4 counts** (some read 5).

She has almost certainly been saying **lā** at 2 counts her whole life. Fix this first;
it is the most audible change in the whole kalima and it makes the sentence sound
immediately different to her own ear, which is exactly the motivation you want on day one
of the unit.

### 2. إِلٰهَ — [[Madd-Tabii]]

The small vertical stroke over the lām (**ٰ**) is a *dagger alif*. It is a full alif and
it is a natural madd: **2 counts, no more.** Having just stretched *lā* to four, she will
want to stretch this to four as well. She must not. Four then two, in adjacent words —
that contrast is the drill.

### 3. إِلَّا — shadda, then [[Madd-Tabii]] again

The lām carries a shadda: hold it, one lām landing and one lām leaving, from the same
makhraj. Then fatḥa + alif = 2 counts. Not four — there is no hamzah after it; the next
thing is the name of Allah.

### 4. إِلَّا اللَّهُ — [[Hamzat-Wasl]] and [[Lam-Jalalah]]

Two rules land on one word, and this word is the spine of the whole unit.

**The alif of الله is a waṣl hamzah.** Joined to what precedes it, it is not pronounced at
all: *illa-llāhu*, one continuous sound. If she says *illā ʾAllāhu* with a glottal stop,
that is the same error the whole of 2-11 was built to remove (see [[2-11-Phrases]]),
reappearing in a sentence she never thought to apply it to.

**Now the lām of the name of Allah.** This is the single most-repeated rule in Unit 4, so
teach it properly once, here, and every later lesson can simply point back:

> **The lām of ٱللَّه is heavy (mufakhkham) after a fatḥa or a ḍamma, and light
> (muraqqaq) after a kasra.**

Look at what precedes it. In **إِلَّا اللَّهُ** the preceding vowel is the **fatḥa** of
*illā* → **heavy**. A full, thick, back-of-the-mouth lām: *illa-LLĀHu*. See [[Tafkhim]]
and [[Tarqiq]] for what heavy and light actually mean in the mouth.

The dagger alif in **اللَّٰه** is again a natural madd — 2 counts.

### 5. مُحَمَّدٌ — [[Ghunnah]]

The mīm carries a shadda. A **mīm mushaddadah** is held for **2 counts of ghunnah** — a
nasal sound resonating in the nose, not in the mouth. Prove it: pinch her nose while she
says it. The sound will change. If it does not change, there is no ghunnah.

*mu-ḥam-mad* — the doubled mīm is where the two counts live.

### 6. مُحَمَّدٌ رَسُولُ — [[Idgham-Bila-Ghunnah]]

The best rule in this kalima and the one most people get wrong.

**مُحَمَّدٌ** ends in tanwīn ḍamm. The next word begins with **ر**. Tanwīn (or nūn
sākinah) followed by ر or ل → **idghām without ghunnah**: the nūn sound is *deleted* and
merged into the rāʾ, with **no nasal residue at all**.

*muḥammadur-rasūl* — **not** *muḥammadun rasūl*, and **not** *muḥammadunr rasūl* with a
hum in between.

This is the rule where "without ghunnah" has to be enforced physically, because idghām
*with* ghunnah ([[Idgham-Maal-Ghunnah]]) is far more common and the mouth defaults to it.
Pinch test again: for *muḥammadur*, pinching the nose should change **nothing**.

### 7. رَسُولُ — [[Ra-Tafkhim]] and [[Madd-Tabii]]

The rāʾ carries a fatḥa → **heavy**. Rāʾ is heavy after fatḥa and ḍamma, light after
kasra ([[Ra-Tarqiq]]); you will do the light case in 4-08.

ḍamma + wāw = natural madd, 2 counts.

### 8. رَسُولُ اللَّهِ — [[Lam-Jalalah]], heavy again

The preceding vowel is the **ḍamma** of *rasūlu* → **heavy** by the same rule as §4.
Point that out explicitly: two occurrences of the name in one sentence, both heavy, but
for two different reasons — one after a fatḥa, one after a ḍamma.

### 9. Stopping at the end — [[Madd-Arid-Lissukun]]

The kalima is recited as one unit and stopped at the end. When you stop on **اللَّهِ**,
the final hāʾ takes a sukūn: *rasūlu-llāh*. Now look at what is immediately before that
hāʾ — the dagger alif, a madd letter. **A madd letter followed by a letter made sākin by
stopping** is madd ʿāriḍ lis-sukūn: **2, 4 or 6 counts**, her choice, but consistent.

She will almost certainly cut it at 2 and clip the hāʾ. Four is the natural length for
this phrase.

### 10. The optional stop — [[Qalqalah-Kubra]]

If she stops for breath at **مُحَمَّدٌ** — and beginners often do — the dāl becomes
sākin at the stop, and dāl is a qalqalah letter: *muḥammad-d*, with a clean bounce. That
is [[Qalqalah-Kubra]], the "greater" qalqalah of a stopped final letter, and it is
stronger than the [[Qalqalah-Sughra]] she met mid-word.

But note the better teaching point: **stopping there also destroys the idghām in §6.**
The rules are not independent of the breath plan. Say so.

## Drills

**Drill 1 — the rule-spotting pass.** Do not recite. Read the sentence silently, put your
finger on each place where a rule lands, and *name the rule out loud*. Nine stops. If you
cannot name it, say "I don't know" and move on — we will come back. Do this before you
ever recite it today.

**Drill 2 — four then two.** Just *lā ilāha*, twenty times. Four counts on the first
alif, two on the dagger alif. Count on your fingers where I can see them. If both come
out the same length, you have not got it, however good it sounds.

**Drill 3 — the pinch.** Say *muḥammadun* (with the nūn) and pinch your nose: the sound
changes. Now say *muḥammadur-rasūl* correctly and pinch: nothing should change. Alternate
until the difference is obvious to you without me telling you.

**Drill 4 — heavy and heavier.** *illa-LLĀHu* … *rasūlu-LLĀH*. Say them back to back.
Both heavy. Both full. Nothing in between them thins out.

**Drill 5 — the join.** *illa-llāhu*, glued, with no stop before the name. Ten times. Then
the whole sentence.

**Drill 6 — the recitation pass.** Now recite the whole kalima, one breath, at half your
normal speed. Then again at your normal speed. If a rule collapses at normal speed, that
rule is not yours yet — tell me which one collapsed. That report is worth more to me than
a clean recitation.

## Listen-for

**Listen for:** one unbroken sentence, four counts on the opening *lā*, two full and heavy
lāms of the name, no nūn before *rasūl*, and a stretched final *-llāh*.

- **The mistake:** *lā* at two counts. **Why it happens:** twenty years of muscle memory
  and no one has ever asked for more. It is not ignorance of the rule — she may be able
  to recite the rule of madd munfaṣil perfectly — it is that she has never connected the
  rule to *this* sentence, because this sentence was never in the category "text you apply
  tajweed to."
  **Say:** *"Same rule you use in the Qurʾān. Madd letter, then a hamzah, next word. Four.
  Give me four."*

- **The mistake:** *muḥammadun rasūl*, the nūn fully pronounced.
  **Why it happens:** the tanwīn is *written*, and she has spent all of Unit 2 learning to
  read what is written. Also: this is how it is said in every speech, every naṣīd, every
  time anyone says it out loud around her. She is imitating a room, not misreading a page.
  **Say:** *"There is no n. The n has gone into the r. muḥammadur — hold the r, then
  rasūl."*

- **The mistake:** a glottal stop before the name of Allah — *illā ʾAllāh*.
  **Why it happens:** the alif is on the page and she is reading it. Exactly the 2-11
  error, in new clothes.
  **Say:** *"Step over the alif. illa-llāhu, one word."*

- **The mistake:** a thin, light lām in the name of Allah, particularly on the second one.
  **Why it happens:** the ḍamma of *rasūlu* is a high, front vowel to her ear and the
  tongue stays forward into the lām. Fatigue at the end of the sentence does the rest.
  **Say:** *"Fill your mouth. The name is heavy here — say it as if it is coming from the
  back of your throat."*

- **The mistake:** the final *-llāh* clipped to nothing, hāʾ swallowed.
  **Why it happens:** she is stopping, and stopping feels like ending, and ending feels
  like getting quieter. The madd ʿāriḍ is the first casualty of the last breath.
  **Say:** *"The last word is the longest word, not the shortest. Four counts, then the
  h."*

## Homework

1. The whole kalima, five times after each ṣalāh, **counting the madds on your fingers.**
   Not from memory-speed — at the speed where you can count.
2. *lā ilāha* alone, four-then-two, twenty times a day. This is the one drill that will
   not fix itself.
3. Write out the nine rule-stops from Drill 1 on a card, in order, in English. Bring the
   card. In 4-02 you will add to it, not rewrite it.
4. Listen to yourself once on your phone. Play it back and mark on the card every place
   the rule did not happen. Bring that too.
