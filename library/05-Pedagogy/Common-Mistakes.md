# Common Mistakes

The remediation track. This is **a separate strand, not a footnote inside each
lesson** — an adult student arrives with existing habits, and correcting a habit is
a different teaching job from introducing a fact. Lessons introduce; this track
diagnoses and repairs.

## 0. Read this first: the Uzbek caveat

**No phonetic study of Uzbek-L1 learners of Arabic was found.** Two research passes
searched for one and returned only alphabet- and script-history material. Every
prediction below about *this* student's likely errors is inferred from documented
Persian, Turkish and Urdu analogues — languages with which Uzbek shares the relevant
gaps (no interdentals, no emphatic series, no pharyngeals) and, crucially, the same
transmission route: Arabic vocabulary reached Uzbek largely **through Persian**,
which had already collapsed these distinctions before passing them on.

Treat this list as **a set of hypotheses for the teacher to verify in lesson 1**,
not as settled fact. Run a diagnostic reading, listen for each item below, and then
rewrite the priority order from what you actually heard. In particular, whether ض
surfaces as *z* or as *d* could genuinely go either way depending on who taught the
student as a child.

One documented advantage worth knowing before you start: Uzbek has phonemic /q/,
/χ/ and /ʁ/. If that transfers, [[Qaf]], [[Kha]] and [[Ghayn]] — the letters that
defeat English and Urdu speakers — may need far less work here than the generic
non-Arab error lists suggest. Check early; if true, it buys sessions to spend on the
throat and the emphatics instead.

## 1. Priority errors, in citation-strength order

These five are the drill-design drivers. They are ordered by how strongly the
sources name them, not by how often this particular student may make them.

### 1. ح vs خ vs ه — the single most-cited non-Arab error

[[Ha-throat]] (ح) is mid-throat, unvoiced, with *hams* — breathy airflow, like
fogging a mirror. It has **no counterpart in English, Turkish, Uzbek, Persian or
Urdu**, so it is not a sound the student can approximate from anything they already
own; it has to be built. The two failure directions are backward into [[Kha]] (too
high in the throat, adding friction) and forward into [[Ha-soft]] (too shallow,
losing the throat squeeze). All three commonly collapse into one generic "h".

*Correction cue:* ح is a squeeze from the middle of the throat with no voicing and
no rasp. Contrast all three in immediate succession, never one at a time — the
category only forms against its neighbours.

### 2. ض — the hardest letter in Arabic for every non-native

[[Dad]] has no light counterpart to lean on and one property no other letter has:
**istiṭālah**, the lateral elongation along the side of the tongue against the
upper molars. See [[Istitalah]]. The documented error is weak or entirely absent
istiṭālah — the letter surfaces as a plain [[Dal]] or as a *z*.

This is explicitly documented as universal among non-natives, not an Uzbek defect.
Ibn al-Jazari gives it **an entire chapter of [[Muqaddimah-Jazariyyah]]** with word
lists, which is why this course gives it a whole lesson (3-07) rather than a
paragraph.

### 3. The ذ/ز/ظ/ض collapse to "z"

Documented specifically for **Persian, Turkish and Urdu speakers**: the emphatic
"D" of ض exists almost nowhere else and becomes just another way to say *z*, and
[[Dhal]] (ذ, voiced "th") and [[Zay]] (ز, plain z) merge alongside it, with
[[Za-heavy]] (ظ) joining them. Four distinct letters, one output sound.

Test all four in minimal-contrast drill words **early** — Unit 1's ذ/ز/ر shape
family is the natural place — and flag any collapse as the primary target. Note for
reassurance: ظ and ض are merged in some Arabic dialects too, so this is a genuinely
hard cluster, not a mark of poor learning.

### 4. ع substituted with ء

[[Ayn]] is a muscular squeeze at the middle of the throat. [[Hamza]] is a plain
glottal stop, deeper and simply closed. Flattening ع into ء — or dropping it
entirely — is the standard error wherever the L1 has no pharyngeals, which includes
Uzbek. A second variant: cutting the sound off too early when ع carries sukūn.

The classic contrast pair is ʿalīm / alīm — same frame, one letter apart, both
extremely frequent, and the meanings are unrelated.

### 5. Tafkhīm bleeding — and the alif that has no ruling of its own

Two errors that look opposite but come from the same misunderstanding of
[[Tafkhim]] and [[Tarqiq]].

*Bleeding inward:* the student produces the emphatic consonant heavy, then
immediately lightens the vowel after it. Heaviness must carry through **the whole
syllable**, including any following alif of madd.

*Bleeding outward:* once a student discovers tafkhīm they over-apply it — heaviness
spreads across the word, or onto neighbouring light letters. Watch [[Hamza]] and
[[Ha-soft]] next to emphatics especially; both get wrongly thickened. Vowel-harmony
habits in a Turkic L1 may make this spread worse, since harmony is exactly a
"spread a feature across the word" rule.

**The governing fact:** *alif al-madd has no independent tafkhīm/tarqīq ruling at
all.* It fully inherits from the letter before it. See [[Istila]]. Students who
learn this as a rule about alif get it wrong; students who learn it as "alif has no
opinion — ask the letter before it" get it right.

## 2. The rest of the letter list

Lower in citation strength, still worth a listen-for entry on every lesson.

| Error | Detail |
|---|---|
| **ق → ك** | [[Qaf]] flattened to a plain *k*. Meaning-changing: qāla ("he said") becomes kāla ("he measured"). The most-cited single letter error worldwide — though Uzbek's native /q/ may spare this student. |
| **ث ↔ ذ** | [[Tha]] (unvoiced "th") produced as [[Dhal]] (voiced "th") or the reverse; or both flattened to *s*/*z*. |
| **ص → س** | [[Sad]] read as a plain [[Seen]], losing the full-mouth thick hiss. ص carries *ṣafīr* and tafkhīm together; س has only the whistle. |
| **ط → ت** | [[Ta-heavy]] read as a plain [[Ta]] — losing the jahr + shiddah + istiʿlāʾ + iṭbāq stack. The reverse over-correction (an exaggerated growl) is just as common once the student notices the letter. |
| **ظ → ذ** | [[Za-heavy]] read as [[Dhal]] — right articulation, no heaviness. |
| **غ ↔ خ** | [[Ghayn]] and [[Kha]] share an upper-throat origin and differ only in voicing; ghayn also drifts toward [[Qaf]]. Both must stay fricatives, never stops. |
| **ه weak or heavy** | [[Ha-soft]] made too breathy to carry, or wrongly thickened next to an emphatic. |
| **ر flat everywhere** | [[Ra]] given one plain tap in all positions, with no heavy realisation. See [[Ra-Tafkhim]] and [[Ra-Tarqiq]] — this is the rule the flat tap is quietly breaking. |

## 3. Rules-phase errors

These arrive with Unit 3 and are mostly **Laḥn Khafi** — they degrade quality
without changing meaning — with the exception of madd length, which is Jali. See
[[Assessment]].

**Ghunnah with no nasal resonance.** The student produces [[Noon|ن]] and [[Meem|م]] "from the mouth",
with the nose doing nothing. [[Ghunnah]] must audibly buzz through the nose for two
counts.
*Self-check — the nose pinch:* recite an [[Idgham-Maal-Ghunnah|idghām-with-ghunnah]] or an [[Ikhfa-Haqiqi|ikhfāʾ]] word while
pinching the nose shut. If the sound does not change, there was no ghunnah in it.
This is one of the very few tajweed features a student can genuinely verify alone,
so teach it early and use it constantly. See also [[Ghunnah-sifah]].

**Defaulting to iẓhār because it is easiest.** The most common stumbling block once
the noon rules begin: the student pronounces everything clearly and unaltered,
because that requires changing nothing. The mirror error is merging everything into
idghām. Both are symptoms of the same gap — the student has not internalised that
the *following letter* selects the rule. Feedback should always name the following
letter and its class, not just mark the answer wrong: see [[Izhar-Halqi]],
[[Idgham-Maal-Ghunnah]], [[Idgham-Bila-Ghunnah]], [[Iqlab]], [[Ikhfa-Haqiqi]].

**Rushing or flattening madd.** Called out across the sources as one of the most
common and audible beginner errors: the elongation is cut short, or not made at
all, or applied at a length the student never actually counts. Wrong madd length is
**Laḥn Jali** — it changes the word. Uzbek has no phonemic vowel length, so 2 vs 4
vs 6 counts has no anchor in the student's first language at all; expect systematic
under-lengthening, especially of [[Madd-Munfasil]], and drill the count explicitly
rather than by feel. See [[Madd-Tabii]], [[Madd-Muttasil]], [[Madd-Lazim]],
[[Madd-Arid-Lissukun]].

**Qalqalah as an added vowel.** The bounce of [[Qalqalah-Sughra]] and
[[Qalqalah-Kubra]] becomes a small extra vowel — "ab-uh" — instead of a clean
echo with no vowel colour. Related: epenthesis. Inserting a vowel to break a
consonant cluster is documented for Arabic loanwords in Uzbek, and the same reflex
breaks sukūn, idghām and qalqalah alike.

**Lām of the divine name.** Failing to switch [[Lam-Jalalah]] between heavy and
light according to the preceding vowel. High-frequency word, high-visibility error.

**Final ḥarakah errors when stopping.** Reading a case ending that should have been
dropped at a stop, or adding a vowel where sukūn belongs. This is why practical
stopping is taught in Unit 2 rather than left to [[Waqf-Signs]] in Unit 3.7.

## 4. How to use this track

1. **Lesson 1 is a diagnostic.** Read, listen, and mark which of the above are
   actually present. Do not assume the list.
2. **Re-rank from evidence.** The order in §1 is citation strength, not this
   student's difficulty. After the diagnostic, the student's own top three go first.
3. **Contrast, never isolation.** A letter category forms against its neighbours.
   Drill ح against خ against ه, ض against د against ز — not one letter at a time.
4. **Log every correction against its letter or rule**, per [[Assessment]] §2, so
   the pattern is visible by session five rather than session twenty.
