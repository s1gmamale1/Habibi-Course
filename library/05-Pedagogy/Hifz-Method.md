# Hifz Method

The memorisation strand runs in parallel with the rules strand across all 36 Unit 3
lessons. Revision lessons pause new *rules*; they never pause hifz.

Scope: **al-Fātiḥa plus the last ten surahs — 11 items.** Small enough that the
whole classical machinery can be run properly rather than approximated.

## 1. Sabaq · Sabqi · Manzil

Every hifz tradition worldwide uses the same three-tier structure. Only the
vocabulary shifts by region; the underlying new / recent / old split is constant.
That convergence is the reason to adopt it as-is rather than invent something.

| Tier | Meaning | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Sabaq** ("lesson") | The new portion memorised *today* | Brought fresh to the teacher each session |
| **Sabqi** ("recent") | The past few days' to two weeks' new material | Bridges short-term to long-term; still fragile, reviewed every session |
| **Manzil** ("old") | Everything past the recent window | Long-term maintenance against forgetting; rotating cycle |

## 2. The numbers

**20–40 total repetitions** for a new portion, within the session. The method
behind the number: read the new portion **three times from the page** with full
attention to pronunciation → look away and recite from memory → if you cannot,
find the exact point of failure and repeat *that* → alternate look / no-look until
it holds. A widely-cited variant is the "20×20": twenty reads looking at the text,
twenty more reviewing, forty in total, described as usually sufficient for one
verse.

**10 extra repetitions after any caught error.** This is a distinct step, not
absorbed into the general count. Once a mistake is caught — by the student or the
teacher — the corrected portion gets ten dedicated repetitions before returning to
the full passage. Give it its own line on the homework checklist; folded silently
into "repeat until right", it does not happen.

**Session order is always Manzil / Sabqi *before* new Sabaq.** Revision precedes
new memorisation. The rationale from the sources is cognitive budget: retention
work done on a tired mind is the work that gets skipped, and old material is what
decays if skipped. New material has the student's motivation behind it and survives
being last.

A 30-minute reference session runs roughly: Sabqi first, then Manzil (about 8–10
minutes), then the new Sabaq. Daily practice is the floor; twice daily is better.
30–45 minutes is described as sustainable for adult learners.

## 3. Promotion: Sabqi → Manzil

**Three consecutive clean recitations promotes a surah from Sabqi to Manzil.**

Flag this honestly wherever it appears in the product: **this is this course's own
default. No source gave a number.** Every source that describes the Sabqi→Manzil
transition says "when it is solid" and stops there. A vague rule is unimplementable
and, worse, unenforceable — so we picked a concrete one, wrote it down, and marked
it as ours. The teacher should adjust it from observation; if this student's surahs
keep decaying after promotion, raise it to four or five.

Once promoted, a surah joins the rotating Manzil pool. With only 11 items, a simple
**two-surahs-per-day rotation** covers the whole pool every five to six days —
no need for the seven-section split that programmes use once a student holds
multiple juzʾ.

## 4. Order: al-Fātiḥa, then 114 → 105

**al-Fātiḥa first**, then an-Nās backward toward al-Fīl — 114, 113, 112, … 105.
Shortest and most prayer-relevant first.

Two independent confirmations that this is standard practice rather than an ad hoc
choice:

- The general beginner-hifz literature converges on "Fātiḥa, then Juzʾ ʿAmmā from
  the end, in increasing length." One source orders the very shortest three-āyah
  surahs first and then the rest by length, which is a refinement of the same
  principle, not a contradiction — the last ten surahs are already nearly sorted by
  length in reverse muṣḥaf order.
- **[[Muallimi-Soniy]]'s own reading series runs exactly 114 → 96 in reverse muṣḥaf
  order.** The course the student's own tradition produces already teaches this
  sequence, which matters more here than any English-language blog.

The cited benefit is motivational and practical at once: short surahs give fast
wins and are immediately usable in ṣalāh.

## 5. Pacing across 36 lessons

**Budget lesson-segments in proportion to a surah's length, not one surah per
lesson.** This heuristic comes from the same Uzbek channel, and it matters: the
eleven items are not equal. al-Fātiḥa and al-Fīl are several times the work of
al-Kawthar or an-Naṣr. Allocating one lesson each guarantees the student is bored
for half of them and drowning in the other half.

Count verses and words, then distribute the 36 lessons' hifz segments in
proportion. The mapping in the older `phase-3-tajweed.md` was built for 26 lessons
and must be regenerated.

## 6. Assessment

Hifz is Test 2 of the Final Checkpoint: al-Fātiḥa then 114 → 105 unaided, scored on
**two separate axes** — the Jali/Khafi tajweed scale, and an independent
memorisation-fidelity axis recording omitted, added or transposed words. More than
three examiner corrections in one surah fails **that surah alone**, not the test.
See [[Assessment]] §4.

Keeping the axes apart is what makes the result actionable: clean recall with rough
recitation and clean recitation with shaky recall need opposite remedies, and a
single combined score cannot tell you which one you are looking at.

## 7. Do not begin hifz ahead of reading

Experienced maktab teachers advise against beginning serious memorisation before a
student can read fluently from the text. This course respects that by construction:
the hifz strand runs through Unit 3, after Checkpoint 2 has already established that
the student can read unseen voweled text unaided. See [[Methodology]].

The corollary for daily work: the student memorises **from the page**, reading it,
not from audio alone. Audio is for checking, and for the tajweed the page cannot
show.
